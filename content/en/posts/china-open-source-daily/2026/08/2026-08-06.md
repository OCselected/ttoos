## China Open Source Daily — 2026-08-06

### 🏗️ Organizational Innovation: DeepSeek's Vision-Driven, No-KPI Model Challenges China's 996 Culture

**1. Fortune (August 1) and Geopolitechs (July 23): DeepSeek Founder Liang Wenfeng Reveals a Radically Different Organizational Model**

On August 1, 2026, Fortune published a major article on DeepSeek founder Liang Wenfeng's organizational philosophy, drawn from a nearly four-hour internal discussion session transcribed by Tencent Technology. This follows the July 23 publication by Geopolitechs of "64 Quotes from DeepSeek's Investor Call," which provided the first comprehensive English-language synthesis of Liang's May 20 investor meeting. Together, these two sources reveal an organizational model that is radically different from both the Chinese 996 norm and the Silicon Valley venture-backed startup model.

**The core organizational claims:**

- **"We don't really have an organization — we're driven by a vision, organized by a vision."** Liang stated that the company's vision "isn't even written down. We've never written anything." The company operates on consensus, not hierarchy: "My authority inside the company is built on consensus."

- **No KPIs, no overtime, half the day unallocated.** "No one manages them. No KPIs," Liang said. "We hope 'formal work' doesn't take more than half of an employee's time. The other half is unallocated — they can do whatever they want." Liang attributed this to two reasons: "First, research needs a relaxed environment; if you push too hard, you can't do research. Second, we're very focused, which means we do very few things."

- **Open source as identity, not strategy.** "Even our strongest model will be open source, because I don't see what's good about closed source." Liang distinguished DeepSeek's open-source approach from competitors: "Zhipu also open-sources, but their open source isn't the same as ours. Their open source has a 'being chased' feeling — they don't see it as the point. For us, it is the point."

- **Restraint as pricing strategy.** DeepSeek's API pricing aims only to "recoup hardware costs in ten months" — far below profit-maximizing levels. "If you want a 100× profit, then open source does affect that. But if we only make, say, a 6× profit, open source doesn't really hurt."

- **AGI as the only goal; commercialization as byproduct.** "We do AGI to do AGI. We just happen to produce something we can commercialize, so we use it... Both consumer and enterprise are side products on the road to AGI."

**Institutional significance: DeepSeek's organizational model is a genuine institutional innovation — and a direct challenge to both Chinese and American AI industry norms.**

From an institutional economics perspective, DeepSeek's model represents a **third way** between two dominant paradigms:

First, **the Chinese 996 model** — exemplified by Alibaba, Tencent, and JD.com — relies on long hours, hierarchical management, and KPI-driven performance. Jack Ma famously called 996 a "great blessing." This model has produced enormous commercial success but is increasingly criticized for burnout, talent waste, and social instability. The Chinese state outlawed 996 in 2021, but the culture persists de facto. DeepSeek's model demonstrates that frontier AI research can be achieved *without* the 996 grind — undermining the cultural assumption that long hours are a necessary condition for Chinese tech success.

Second, **the Silicon Valley VC model** — exemplified by OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind — relies on massive capital investment, elite credentials, and shareholder-value maximization. This model filters heavily on PhD credentials, publication counts, and prior industry track records. DeepSeek deliberately inverts this: its hiring standard is "passion and curiosity, not prior experience," and core roles are filled primarily by fresh graduates from Chinese universities. A Stanford-Hoover Institution analysis found that despite the youth of the team, DeepSeek authors averaged 61 published papers and 1,059 citations per researcher — young but not untrained.

Third, **DeepSeek's model is vision-driven, consensus-based, and deliberately non-commercial.** The company is funded entirely by High-Flyer Quant (~$8 billion AUM), with no external VC. Liang holds approximately 1% of equity; 99% is held by the High-Flyer partnership. This capital structure frees DeepSeek from the pressure to maximize short-term revenue — and from the governance demands of external investors. The result is an organization that can pursue AGI as a research mission rather than a commercial product.

The critical institutional question is **whether this model is replicable or unique to DeepSeek's specific circumstances.** The combination of (a) a wealthy quant fund parent providing patient capital, (b) a founder who is personally wealthy and ideologically committed to open source, (c) a small team of elite-but-young researchers, and (d) the absence of external VC pressure is difficult to reproduce. If DeepSeek goes public — as the IPO preparations suggest it will — the capital structure will change, and the vision-driven model will come under pressure from public-market governance requirements. The transition from private vision-driven organization to publicly traded corporation is the most significant institutional test facing DeepSeek.

### ⚖️ Legal & Regulatory: CAC's 94-Article Internet Rulebook Draft — The Biggest Rewrite in 25 Years

**2. Geopolitechs (July 3): China's Internet Regulation Draft Introduces Dedicated AI Chapter, AI Agents, and Gig Worker Protections**

On July 3, 2026, the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) released a draft revision of the Measures for the Administration of Internet Information Services for public consultation. The original 2000 regulation — State Council Order No. 292, signed by then-Premier Zhu Rongji — consisted of 27 articles and established the basic ICP filing system that has governed China's internet for 25 years. The new draft has expanded to **94 articles**, nearly doubling the 54-article draft published (but never adopted) in 2021.

**Key structural changes:**

- **Regulatory authority reallocation.** The 2000 regulation was led by the then-Ministry of Information Industry (MIIT's predecessor). The new draft explicitly identifies the **CAC as the primary authority**, responsible for coordinating national cybersecurity policy and supervising online content. MIIT retains responsibility mainly for industrial matters. The institutional arrangement that has gradually emerged over the past decade — "the CAC leads, MIIT manages industry" — is now formally embedded in administrative law.

- **Unified approval framework replacing the dual-track ICP system.** For 25 years, China's internet operated under a dual-track system: commercial services required an ICP license, non-commercial services only needed an ICP filing. The new draft largely removes this distinction, introducing a unified approval framework. ICP approvals can be revoked if services fail to commence within two years.

- **Dedicated AI chapter — "intelligent information services."** For the first time, generative AI is comprehensively regulated through an administrative regulation rather than lower-level ministerial rules. The draft introduces requirements covering training data provenance, mandatory labeling of AI-generated content, prohibitions on tampering with labels, algorithm registration, and the option for users to disable personalized recommendations. Notably, **the term "AI agents" appears for the first time in an administrative regulation** — a significant institutional milestone following the July 15 AI agent regulatory framework.

- **Gig worker algorithmic protections.** The draft requires algorithms affecting delivery riders, ride-hailing drivers, and other platform workers to be "transparent, fair, and reasonable." Significant changes to algorithms governing recruitment, dispatching, or performance evaluation must involve consultation with affected workers. This is the first time that algorithmic management of gig workers has been addressed at the administrative regulation level.

- **Dual-liability regime.** Previously, internet-related violations primarily resulted in penalties against companies. The new draft introduces dual liability: both companies and responsible individuals may be sanctioned. Individuals directly responsible face fines of up to **RMB 1 million** and may be barred from serving as directors, supervisors, or senior executives. Corporate fines increase from RMB 1 million to **RMB 10 million**.

- **Cross-border and VIE structure scrutiny.** Articles 91, 92, and 38 narrow the regulatory grey zone for foreign-invested and cross-border internet services. Being incorporated abroad or hosting servers overseas will no longer shield a platform from Chinese regulation if it targets Chinese users. For VIE-structured companies, regulators may look beyond legal structures to examine who actually controls the business — particularly relevant for search, content distribution, recommendation algorithms, and generative AI.

**Institutional significance: The 94-article draft represents the most comprehensive institutionalization of China's internet governance framework to date.**

The draft does five things simultaneously: (1) it consolidates obligations previously scattered across the Cybersecurity Law, Data Security Law, Personal Information Protection Law, and numerous ministerial regulations into a single administrative framework; (2) it formally codifies the CAC's central coordinating role; (3) it brings AI, AI agents, deepfakes, recommendation algorithms, livestreaming, and the platform economy under a unified regulatory framework; (4) it extends personal accountability through dual-liability provisions; and (5) it addresses the social costs of platform economy through gig worker algorithmic protections.

From an institutional economics perspective, the gig worker provision is particularly significant. The August 3 briefing covered The Guardian's reporting on the 320 million gig workers in China (44% of the workforce) and the robotaxi paradox — the state simultaneously promoting AI deployment and guaranteeing social stability. The draft's requirement that algorithmic changes affecting gig workers involve consultation is an institutional response to this tension. It represents a formal recognition that algorithmic management is not merely a technical process but a governance issue with distributional consequences.

The dual-liability regime also marks a significant shift. By holding individuals — not just corporate entities — responsible for violations, the draft follows the pattern of China's financial and securities regulation, where accountability has shifted from institutions to executives. This creates a new incentive structure for platform executives: personal liability for regulatory violations changes the calculus of non-compliance.

The timing is institutionally significant. The draft was published on July 3, just two weeks before the AI agent regulations (July 15) and two weeks before Xi's WAIC speech (July 17) positioning China as a global AI governance leader. The 94-article draft is the legal infrastructure that underpins the governance framework China is promoting internationally through WAICO. It demonstrates that China's AI governance claims are not merely rhetorical — they are backed by the most comprehensive internet regulatory framework in the world.

### 🏛️ Community Governance: CCF China Open Source Conference 2026 Announces Subforums

**3. CCF Open Source Development Committee: 2026 CCF China Open Source Conference Subforums Revealed**

The **CCF (China Computer Federation) Open Source Development Technology Committee** has been progressively announcing subforums for the **2026 CCF China Open Source Conference (中国开源大会)**, with several subforum topics published in late July and early August via the committee's WeChat account. The announcements reveal the institutional priorities of China's academic open source community.

**Key subforums announced:**

- **"Trustworthy Software Research and Development and Maintenance Driven by Large Models"** (大模型驱动的开源软件可信研发与维护) — This subforum focuses on using LLMs to improve the trustworthiness, security, and maintainability of open source software. The framing of "trustworthy" (可信) is significant: it reflects a persistent concern in Chinese open source policy about the reliability and security of open source components, particularly in the context of supply chain attacks and software vulnerabilities.

- **"Open Source Software Development in the Agent Era: Empowerment and Challenges"** (智能体时代的开源软件开发：赋能与挑战) — This subforum addresses the intersection of AI agents and open source software development, examining how agentic AI tools are changing the way open source software is written, maintained, and governed. This is a forward-looking topic that places the Chinese academic community at the intersection of two major trends: the rise of AI agents and the evolution of open source development practices.

- **A subforum featuring 瀚博半导体 (HANBO Semiconductor) executive Huang Shufu** on "Using Domestic GPU Computing Power to Support Embodied Intelligence Real-Time Simulation and Large-Scale World Model Applications" — This subforum highlights the intersection of domestic chip development, embodied intelligence, and open source, reflecting the institutional priority of building domestic computing power alternatives to Nvidia.

**Institutional significance: The CCF subforum selections reveal the academic community's institutional priorities for Chinese open source.**

The CCF — as China's premier computer science academic organization — plays a unique institutional role. Unlike the OpenAtom Foundation (which is a state-chartered foundation focused on project incubation and industry policy) or COPU (which is an industry promotion alliance), the CCF represents the academic research community. Its subforum choices reflect what the academic community considers the most important open source research questions.

The focus on "trustworthy software" and "AI agents in open source development" reveals two institutional priorities. First, the academic community is concerned about the governance gap in open source software — the "trustworthy" framing reflects a desire to bring scientific rigor and institutional accountability to open source software quality. Second, the community is actively exploring how AI agents will transform the open source development process itself — a topic that has significant implications for the future of collaborative software development.

The inclusion of a domestic GPU maker (HANBO Semiconductor) in the subforum lineup reflects the institutional priority of building domestic computing alternatives — a priority that has intensified since US export controls on advanced chips. The framing of "domestic GPU computing power" for "embodied intelligence" and "world models" connects open source to the broader industrial policy goal of technology sovereignty.

### 🏗️ Ecosystem Building: Shanghai's Open Source Platform Plan and OpenAtom's Hong Kong Expansion

**4. Shanghai's Plan for China's First Open-Source Platform Targeting Global Users; OpenAtom Foundation Expands into Hong Kong**

Two developments in Chinese open source ecosystem building merit attention:

**Shanghai's open source platform plan (originally announced December 2025, implementation ongoing in 2026):**

Shanghai has announced plans to launch and operate **China's first open-source platform aimed at overseas users**, with the goal of establishing 1-2 internationally influential open source communities, incubating more than **200 high-quality open source projects**, and attracting over **3 million developers worldwide** within 3-5 years. The plan includes funding subsidies of up to **CNY 20 million (USD 2.9 million)** per project for foundational tools development, and incentives of up to **CNY 5 million** for open source contributions recognized as "encouraged innovation behavior." The plan specifically targets AI, intelligent chips, high-end software, the metaverse, and RISC-V architecture.

**OpenAtom Foundation's Hong Kong expansion (July 29):**

The OpenAtom Foundation hosted its "Park Tour" (园区行) event in Hong Kong on July 29, focused on openEuler's internationalization. The event, co-organized with the Hong Kong Logistics and Supply Chain Multi-tech R&D Centre (LSCM), gathered Hong Kong government, enterprise, and university stakeholders to discuss mainland-Hong Kong technology collaboration and position Hong Kong as a bridge connecting mainland open source projects to global markets.

**Institutional significance: These two developments represent different models of internationalization for Chinese open source.**

Shanghai's platform plan represents a **state-directed internationalization model** — the municipal government is directly funding and building infrastructure designed to attract global developers to China-based open source projects. This is a top-down approach: the government identifies open source as strategic infrastructure, allocates funding, sets targets, and builds the platform. The plan's explicit goal of targeting "overseas users" and attracting "3 million developers worldwide" makes it a state-directed attempt to compete with GitHub and other international platforms for developer mindshare.

OpenAtom's Hong Kong expansion represents a **bridge model** — using Hong Kong's unique position as a semi-autonomous financial and technology hub with international connectivity to route mainland open source projects into global markets. The choice of openEuler (an operating system) as the pilot project is significant: openEuler is one of OpenAtom's flagship projects, and its internationalization through Hong Kong represents a test case for whether mainland-governed open source projects can gain international adoption through a Hong Kong intermediary.

The contrast between these two models is instructive. Shanghai's plan is about building a new platform from scratch — a "build it and they will come" approach. OpenAtom's Hong Kong strategy is about leveraging existing institutional bridges — a "route through existing channels" approach. Whether either model succeeds in attracting international developers to Chinese-governed open source projects remains an open question, but the dual strategy reflects the institutional complexity of Chinese open source internationalization.

### 📊 Foundation Dynamics: Apache Software Foundation Sees Surge in China-Contributed Projects

**5. The Stack (August 2026): Apache Foundation Reports 10 of 28 Incubating Projects Originated from Chinese Tech Companies**

The Stack published an analysis of China's growing ties with international open source foundations, reporting that the **Apache Software Foundation (ASF)** has seen a significant rise in projects contributed from China. Of the ASF's 28 current incubating projects, **10 began life at a Chinese tech company**, with Alibaba, Ant Group, and Xiaomi being the biggest contributors. ASF Vice Chair Justin Mclean confirmed the trend.

The article also noted the complexity of this growing relationship: the Chinese government's involvement in open source as a soft power instrument (per the Tony Blair Institute's assessment), murky licensing practices, and state-backed hacking campaigns have raised questions about how other countries should respond to China's growing open source community.

**Institutional significance: The Apache data reveals a structural shift in the direction of open source project contribution — from consumption to production.**

For decades, China's relationship with open source was primarily consumptive: Chinese companies used open source software (Linux, Apache projects, MySQL) but contributed relatively little back. The Apache incubator data — 10 of 28 incubating projects from Chinese companies — represents a structural reversal. Chinese tech companies are now **producing** open source projects at scale and donating them to international foundations for governance.

This shift has significant institutional implications. When a Chinese company donates a project to Apache, it submits to Apache's governance model — which is community-driven, meritocratic, and transparent. This is fundamentally different from the OpenAtom Foundation model, where governance remains under Chinese state-chartered control. The growing pipeline of Chinese-to-Apache donations suggests that Chinese tech companies recognize the value of international foundation governance for their projects' global adoption — even as the Chinese state promotes domestically governed alternatives.

The tension between these two paths — international foundation governance (Apache, Linux Foundation) and domestic state-chartered governance (OpenAtom) — will define the institutional trajectory of Chinese open source. The Apache data suggests that, at the project level, Chinese companies are voting with their code for international governance — even as the state builds domestic alternatives.

### 🔍 Commentary

**The Institutional Architecture of Chinese Open Source: Three Models in Tension**

The stories in this briefing reveal three distinct institutional models of Chinese open source operating simultaneously — and the tensions between them define the trajectory of the ecosystem.

**Model 1: The Vision-Driven Lab (DeepSeek)**

DeepSeek's organizational model — no KPIs, no hierarchy, vision-driven, open source as identity — is an institutional anomaly in the Chinese context. It is made possible by a unique capital structure (quant fund parent, no external VC), a unique founder (personally wealthy, ideologically committed to open source), and a unique team composition (elite-but-young, fresh graduates). The model produces frontier research output at a fraction of the cost and headcount of competitors, but its replicability is questionable.

The DeepSeek model challenges the assumption that Chinese tech success requires 996 culture. It also challenges the assumption that frontier AI requires VC-scale investment and elite-credential hiring. If the model survives the transition to public-market governance (the IPO), it could become a template for a new kind of Chinese tech company — one that is publicly traded but governed by research mission rather than shareholder value maximization. But the IPO process will test whether the vision-driven model can survive the transparency, accountability, and short-term performance pressures of public markets.

**Model 2: The State-Chartered Foundation (OpenAtom, CCF)**

The OpenAtom Foundation and the CCF represent the state-chartered model of open source governance. OpenAtom is a state-chartered foundation that incubates projects, builds industry ecosystems, and promotes open source as a component of industrial policy. The CCF represents the academic community's engagement with open source, with subforums on trustworthy software, AI agents, and domestic computing power.

This model has clear advantages: it can mobilize state resources, align open source with industrial policy, and build infrastructure at scale (witness the 70+ local policies, the Shanghai platform plan, the Hong Kong expansion). But it also has inherent limitations: the state-chartered model carries the credibility discount of state affiliation, the governance constraints of political alignment, and the innovation constraints of top-down direction.

The OpenAtom Foundation's expansion into Hong Kong and the Shanghai platform plan represent attempts to overcome these limitations — by using Hong Kong as an international bridge and by building a platform designed to attract global developers. But both attempts face the same fundamental challenge: international developers and companies may be reluctant to contribute to projects governed under Chinese state-chartered frameworks, particularly given concerns about government involvement, murky licensing, and cybersecurity.

**Model 3: The International Foundation Path (Apache, Linux Foundation)**

The Apache incubator data — 10 of 28 incubating projects from Chinese companies — represents the third model: Chinese companies donating projects to international foundations and submitting to international governance. This model solves the credibility problem (Apache governance is trusted globally) but creates a different challenge: it means that the most successful Chinese open source projects may end up governed outside of China.

The tension between Model 2 (state-chartered domestic foundations) and Model 3 (international foundations) is the central institutional tension in Chinese open source. The state wants to build domestic governance capacity (OpenAtom, Shanghai platform) — but Chinese companies, seeking global adoption for their projects, are choosing international foundations (Apache, Linux Foundation) that offer the governance credibility that domestic alternatives lack.

**The CAC's 94-Article Draft as Meta-Governance**

The CAC's internet rulebook draft is the meta-governance layer that sits above all three models. Regardless of which organizational model a Chinese open source entity uses — vision-driven lab, state-chartered foundation, or international foundation donor — it must comply with the CAC's regulatory framework. The draft's provisions on AI content labeling, algorithm registration, gig worker protections, and dual-liability create a regulatory floor that constrains all Chinese open source activity.

The most significant provision for open source is the AI chapter — the first appearance of "AI agents" in an administrative regulation. This creates a regulatory framework that will govern how Chinese AI companies (including DeepSeek, Moonshot, and others) deploy their models — including open source models. The requirement for training data provenance, AI content labeling, and algorithm registration applies to open source AI models just as it applies to proprietary ones. This means that the "openness" of Chinese open source AI is bounded by regulatory requirements that are more comprehensive than those in any other jurisdiction.

**The Coming Test: IPO Transition**

The coming months will see the first major test of these institutional models: the transition of Chinese AI companies from private to public ownership. DeepSeek's IPO preparation, Moonshot's $35 billion valuation, and the broader IPO rush will force these companies to navigate the tension between their open source missions and their fiduciary duties to public shareholders. The CAC's regulatory framework, the CCF's academic priorities, and the Apache Foundation's governance model will all be tested as these companies go public.

The institutional question is whether the Chinese open source ecosystem can develop governance models that are simultaneously (a) credible enough to attract international developers, (b) compliant with the CAC's regulatory framework, and (c) compatible with the fiduciary duties of publicly traded companies. No model currently satisfies all three constraints — which suggests that the institutional innovation seen in this period is not yet complete.

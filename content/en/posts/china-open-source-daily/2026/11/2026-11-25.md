⚠️ **Editorial note: The open source ecosystem in China operates under a distinct institutional framework — characterized by state-led initiatives, intranet-like boundaries, and top-down governance. Readers should be aware that this context differs from the conventional model common in other regions. The term "open source" as used in Chinese media may refer to practices that diverge from the conventional definition.**

## China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-25

### 🏛️ Policy & Regulation — The Standard Goes Upstream of the Code: Reading the Open Source Standards Working Group

**1. The announcement, in the only place it exists.** On **2026-11-20**, at the **third Open Source Technology Academic Forum (第三届开源技术学术论坛)** held inside the **2026 OpenAtom Developer Conference** in **Zhengzhou**, the **China Electronics Standardization Institute (中国电子技术标准化研究院, known as 电子标准院 / CESI)** and the **OpenAtom Open Source Foundation** jointly announced the establishment of an **Open Source Standards Working Group (开源标准工作组)**. The wording of the announcement is short enough to quote in full, and it should be, because the entire institutional content is in the verbs:

> *"The working group will establish a mechanism for converting de facto open-source standards (建立开源事实标准转化工作机制), forming a virtuous closed loop of 'standards carry open source, open source verifies standards' (形成"标准带开源、开源验标准"的良性闭环), and promoting a batch of high-quality, active open-source technology projects to be converted into standards outcomes that represent industrial consensus (推动一批优质、活跃的开源技术项目转化为产业共识的标准成果)."*

And the forward commitment: *"Next, the Electronics Standards Institute will continue to leverage its professional strengths in standards development, open-source governance and compliance assessment, and will work with the foundation to promote the orderly conversion of mature practice into group, industry, national and international standards (推动成熟实践向团体、行业、国家及国际标准有序转化)."*

**2. Who signed it, and who was in the room that day.** The announcement is carried by **CESI's own WeChat account**, under the title *"电子标准院参加2026开放原子开发者大会"*, and the names attached are institutional rather than personal: **Zhang Gengsheng (张更生)**, deputy party secretary of CESI, appeared at the opening ceremony for the launch of the **OpenAtom Smart Agriculture System Open Source Community (开放原子智农系统开源社区)**. The conference itself was opened by **Wang Song (王崧)**, party secretary of Zhengzhou; **Wang Weiwei (王威伟)** of MIIT's information technology development department; **Zhuang Jianqiu (庄建球)**, mayor of Zhengzhou; **Xie Shaofeng (谢少锋)**, chairman of OpenAtom; and **Lü Jian (吕建)**, CAS academician, who gave the keynote.

**3. What kind of institution CESI is, because the identity determines what the working group can do.** CESI is the **national institute for electronics standardisation**, the body that drafts and maintains the GB and GB/T series in the electronics and IT domain, on which organisations like CAICT and the various MIIT technical committees rely when they need a national standard to cite. It is also the originator of the **Mulan licence family (木兰许可证族)**, whose *"six-piece set"* was **donated to OpenAtom on 2026-06-25** at the same conference series, with CESI's own **Wang Zhipeng (王志鹏)**, director of its software application and service research centre, on stage for the transfer. **The connection is exact and worth stating plainly: the same institute that wrote China's open-source licence family is now convening the group that will decide which open-source projects become standards.**

**4. The precedent that shapes how this should be read, and it is six days old in this series.** On **2026-11-13**, at the **2026 Industrial Operating System Conference** in Shanghai, MIIT's **Industrial Software Standardization Technical Committee** — founded **2026-10-21**, secretariat at MIIT's **Fifth Electronics Research Institute** — **formally launched the establishment of an Industrial Operating System Standards Working Group**, organised around **reference architecture, interconnection, compatibility and adaptation, and test evaluation**, together with a **pilot validation formation** across steel, non-ferrous mining, rail transit and nuclear power. **That group governs a production floor and has no source code.** Today's group has the opposite assignment: it starts from **existing projects** and asks what of them can be turned into a standard. **The two instruments are complementary halves of the same pipeline** — one writes the requirement and hands it down, the other harvests the practice and hands it up — and they were announced **seven days and four hundred kilometres apart** without either announcement referring to the other.

---

**Institutional Analysis & Commentary:**

**This is a small announcement with a large claim inside it, and the claim is about the direction in which the authority flows. The eight characters 标准带开源、开源验标准 describe a loop, but the two halves of the loop are not symmetrical, and the asymmetry is the whole finding.**

**First, credit the thing that is genuinely right, because it is not nothing and it is not the usual failure.** The phrase *"standards carry open source, open source verifies standards"* is, as a design idea, **better than what most of this ecosystem has produced**. It says a standard should not be written in a room and imposed on a project; it should be **tested against a running implementation**, and the implementation should be the thing that proves or falsifies the standard. In the vocabulary this series has used all year: **the loop has a falsification step.** A standard that an open-source project can fail is a standard that can be wrong, and a standard that can be wrong is one that carries information. Compare it to what this series has catalogued — certification regimes where the only passing condition is the certifier's approval, maturity assessments issued by the body that wrote the assessment, an identity code that becomes a licence condition through the phrase *"by reference to the standards"*. **This document proposes a quality-control mechanism, and it is the first one in the corpus that runs in the direction of correction rather than approval.**

**Second, the direction of travel, and it is stated in the source rather than inferred.** *"Promote the orderly conversion of mature practice into group, industry, national and international standards."* Read the sequence: **团标 → 行标 → 国标 → 国际标准.** Four rungs, each one higher in authority and lower in accessibility, and the document calls the movement between them **orderly (有序)** — which in standards language means **sequenced, gated and approved**. Now set that beside the first half of the loop. **"Open source verifies standards" is a technical act: someone runs a test and reports whether it passes.** **"Mature practice converts to national standards" is an administrative act: someone decides that the practice is mature.** Between those two acts sits a body, and the body is CESI. **So the loop is real, but it is a loop with a gate on the output side and no gate on the input side** — every project is eligible to be harvested, and the decision about which harvest reaches the next rung belongs to the standards institute and its committees.

**Third, the institutional economics, stated in the terms that fit, and it is a story about where in the production process the governance attaches.** In **Ostrom's** account of commons governance, the design principles that make a commons durable are about **boundary rules and collective-choice arrangements**: who is a participant, and who decides the rules the participants live under. In this arrangement, **the participant boundary is the project's contributor list, and the collective-choice arrangement is CESI's committee.** The two are not connected. A maintainer who has run a project for five years, reviewed a thousand patches and made every technical decision the project required is **a subject of this pipeline, not a member of it** — the pipeline asks whether their practice is mature, using criteria the pipeline authors. **In Williamson's L2/L3 terms this is the standard move of Chinese open-source institutional design, executed with unusual clarity: the governance instrument is placed upstream of the artefact rather than attached to the community that produces it.** The project generates the practice; the standard captures it; the conversion is administered.

**Fourth, and this is the consequence that matters most for anyone outside China, because "international standards" is in the list and it changes the cost structure of the whole ecosystem.** If a Chinese open-source project's practice is converted to a **GB/T**, an **industry standard** or a **group standard (团体标准)**, and that standard is then proposed at **ISO/IEC JTC 1**, **ITU-T** or **IEEE**, the artefact that reaches the international table is **not the project**. It is **a text about the project, drafted by an institute, carrying the authority of a national standardisation system.** This is a legitimate and universally practised route — every standards body in the world does it, and the Linux Foundation's projects do it constantly. **But it has a specific effect on the commons that nobody in this ecosystem has written about: it converts the project's technical decisions into a national position.** A project that ships under Apache-2.0 with a public issue tracker and a meritocratic commit process has, at that moment, **two channels of influence over its own future**: the technical channel, where a contributor's patch either merges or does not, and the standards channel, where a delegate's national body either does or does not adopt the practice. **The first channel is open to anyone with a pull request. The second is open to anyone with a delegation.** Today's announcement builds the second and describes it as serving the first.

**Fifth, and here the analysis has to be honest about evidence, because the announcement is thin and that thinness is itself a datum.** The whole document is roughly **six hundred characters**. It names **no member organisations**, **no chair**, **no secretariat**, **no work programme**, **no project list**, **no timeline**, and **no publication commitment**. The conference press release from **China News Service**, the **Guangming Daily** and **China Daily** all report the conference — the Zhengzhou city project, the 32 recognised developers, the new communities, the five donated projects — and **none of them mentions the standards working group at all**. Across this run, SearXNG aggregation, direct extraction of the three conference reports and a search of CESI's own channel returned **exactly one source** for this instrument: **the Institute's own WeChat post, supplied through the manual feed**. **This is not a criticism of the group; it is a measurement of its institutional visibility.** A working group with a published membership and a work plan can be assessed, compared and held to account. A working group with eight characters of slogan and no roster cannot be assessed at all, and **in this ecosystem the absence of a roster is a reliable predictor of what the instrument will turn out to be** — this series has now tested that prediction four times without a counterexample.

**Sixth, the two questions that decide everything, and both are cheap to answer.** **Is the working group's membership list published, and does it contain a maintainer of a project that is not a donor to the foundation?** If the standing members are CESI's standardisers plus the foundation's large donors plus the vendors selling into procurement, the loop will produce conformance documents, because that is what a room full of suppliers and buyers writes. If it contains project maintainers with binding votes, it might produce something else. **And: when a project is converted to a standard, does the standard get published under an OSI-approved documentation licence on a public URL, or does it become a purchasable text?** This is the entire difference between **a coordination device** — a written answer strangers can rely on — and **a gate**. The Mulan licence family's own history is the precedent worth watching: it was donated, it is freely available, and it is the single best artefact CESI has put into the commons. **Whether the standards working group inherits that practice or departs from it is observable within one release cycle, and no policy change is required to observe it.**

**Checkable questions.**

**(i) Does the Open Source Standards Working Group publish a member roster, a chair and a secretariat, and does any member hold merge rights in a project the group intends to standardise?** A standards body whose members are the projects' maintainers is a coordination body; a body whose members are their suppliers and buyers is a procurement annex.

**(ii) Are converted standards published as open, freely accessible documents under an OSI-approved licence, or as purchasable texts?** This is the first and almost the only question worth asking, and the answer is visible on one URL.

**(iii) Which projects are in the first batch, and were their maintainers asked?** "推动一批优质、活跃的开源技术项目" is a selection, not a process. The list and the consent of its subjects would tell an outside party what class of thing is being harvested — and the harvest is more consequential than the standard, because the standard can be ignored and the classification cannot.

**(iv) Do any of the resulting standards reach ISO/IEC JTC 1, ITU-T or IEEE, and who holds the delegate seat?** If Chinese open-source practice becomes a national position at an international standards table, the project's maintainers should be at the table. Whether they are is a matter of public record.

### 🏗️ Institutional Change — Five More Projects and the Ratio Nobody Publishes: OpenAtom's Intake Reaches Sixty-Four

**1. The intake, counted.** The **2026 OpenAtom Developer Conference** held in **Zhengzhou** on **2026-11-20** added, in one morning: **five projects formally donated into the foundation's incubation system** — **AISHPerf**, **RoboFusion**, **SpaceAICOS**, **ExusData** and **Kindling** — covering AI compute benchmarking, embodied-intelligence data and fusion frameworks, cloud-native observability and intelligent systems; **two new working committees**, for the **humanoid robot open source community (人形机器人开源社区)** and **OpenLoong**; an **OpenAtom Smart Agriculture System Open Source Community (智农系统开源社区)** launch, attended by CESI's Zhang Gengsheng; **M-Robots OS 3.0**; and an **OpenLET** embodied-intelligence data matrix. Separately at the same conference, the **LoongArch Community** was chartered with **22 founding members**.

**2. The arithmetic, which is the only honest way to state it.** OpenAtom's own account of the **2026-06-25** ecosystem conference — the one where seven projects signed and the Mulan licences were donated — states that the foundation had **cumulatively incubated 59 projects across 18 key fields** since its **June 2020** founding, of which **10 have established working committees** and **two have graduated** (OpenHarmony and openEuler, both in 2025). **Five more on 2026-11-20 puts the portfolio at 64.** That gives two ratios, and both are published by the foundation or derivable from what it publishes: **2 of 64 graduated — 3.1 per cent**; **10 of 64 with a working committee — 15.6 per cent.** For comparison, the previous year's developer conference, on **2025-11-21**, signed **11 projects** and reported **50 incubation-stage projects plus 2 graduated** — so the portfolio grew from **52 to 64 in twelve months, a 23 per cent increase in hosted projects against two graduations.**

**3. The city, because the city is now an instrument too.** Zhengzhou and OpenAtom **launched a joint "open-source city" co-construction project (开源之城共建项目)**, which per the reporting will operate through **a standing cooperation mechanism (常态化合作机制)** across **intelligent terminals, computing clusters and AI**, delivering **training and certification, compatibility assessment, and enterprise open-source governance services**, and converting open-source projects, developers and technology resources into local industrial demand. Zhengzhou puts **nearly 40,000 software and internet enterprises**, an industry scale **above CNY 130 billion**, **68,000 PFLOPS** of installed compute, **73 universities and colleges** with **more than 1.53 million students**, and a **2026 target of CNY 150 billion** in software and IT services on the table. **A June 2026 precedent already exists**: the **OpenLET Zhengzhou working group** was set up in the Zhengzhou High-Tech Zone as the first local node of that community.

**4. The figure MIIT restated, because it is the same figure as last year's conference and it is not a census.** **Wang Weiwei** of MIIT's information technology development department told the conference that China *"contributes more than 2.6 million active developers to the world"* — *"a large and structurally diverse open-source talent pool"* that has *"given rise to a batch of open-source projects with industrial competitiveness and international influence."* He restated the department's three priorities: **support for supply capacity** across incubation, collaborative development and security governance; **layout in frontier fields** with open scenarios and application validation; and **contribution-oriented open-source talent evaluation (以贡献为导向的开源人才评价)** at enterprises and universities. **These are the same figures and the same three priorities this series examined on 2026-11-21**; nothing new was said, and the fact that nothing new was said is worth recording.

**Institutional Analysis & Commentary:**

**This is the fourth consecutive day this series has had to write about OpenAtom's intake, and the analysis has to move past counting, because counting is now the easy part. What follows is the one measurement the foundation could publish tomorrow and does not.**

**First, be fair to what has been built, and it is more than a fair-minded reader of the previous week would expect.** Sixty-four projects across eighteen fields, a foundation supervised by MIIT and audited through statutory annual-inspection filings, **a published recognition methodology** for its 32 annual outstanding developers that this series examined on **2026-11-24** as the first falsifiable number the ecosystem has produced, **a donated and freely available licence family**, and now **a joint city mechanism with an explicit service catalogue** rather than a memorandum of understanding. **An organisation that can be audited from outside is governed differently from one that cannot**, and OpenAtom can be. **The criticism that follows is not that the intake is illegitimate. It is that intake is the only thing being measured, and intake has a denominator that nobody has set.**

**Second, the measurement, stated as the thing it should be.** A foundation that hosts **64 projects** owes the people who depend on them **one table**: for each project, over the last twelve months — **did it ship a release; how many commits; how many contributors; what share of them are employed by the donating company; is there a maintainer who is not; is there a published contribution guide; is the licence OSI-approved; did any patch come from outside the founding organisation.** This series has been running that measurement on individual projects all year, and the results are consistent and bleak: **LoongArch's flagship repository, one outside pull request in forty-three days** (2026-11-21); **OpenAgenet, 488 commits across sixteen repositories in six months, all from one email address, zero outside contributors, 94 npm downloads in thirty days** (2026-11-22); **Tencent's Hy3, Hy4 preview and Hy4 — three releases, five to three commits each, every one from a single account, no outside patch** (2026-11-23). **Those are the projects that publish their repositories.** For most of the 64, no such measurement is possible from outside, because the repository either does not exist in a public forge or its contribution surface is not the project's centre of gravity. **The foundation's filings itemise income and expenditure by project — a cost view. A contribution view would cost one query per project and would change what the next donation means.**

**Third, the institutional economics of intake, and this is the part the foundation's own charter makes difficult.** A foundation that hosts projects is running, in **Coase's** terms, **an asset-specificity sink**: it absorbs legal title, trademark, compliance liability and CI infrastructure, all of which are expensive for any single donor to carry and cheap to hold centrally. That is a real service and it is why foundations exist. **The hazard it introduces is exactly the one that makes vertical integration necessary in Williamson — and it is a governance hazard, not a financial one: the entity holding the commons has no way to tell whether the commons is alive.** A project that is hosted and dead is administratively indistinguishable from a project that is hosted and thriving, **unless someone counts contributions**, and a foundation whose funding comes largely from the companies whose projects it hosts has a structural disinclination to publish a table in which most rows would be thin. **This is not venality and it is worth saying clearly: it is the ordinary bias of any organisation whose intake is its success metric.** The control that fixes it is not moral. It is **the publication of a denominator**, because once the denominator exists, the next question asks itself.

**Fourth, the "open-source city" is the instrument to watch, and it is more interesting than it looks.** Read the service list: **training and certification, compatibility assessment, enterprise open-source governance**. Three services, and all three are **things a local government can procure and a company can bill for**. That is precisely the shape this series identified in Xi'an on **2026-11-11** — three lists converting governance goals into technical requirements, certification criteria and market channels, adaptation vouchers for SMEs, and a loop running from R&D to adaptation to certification to **procurement**, terminating in a city transport contract won by a consortium led by the company that co-hosts the certification centre. **Zhengzhou's version is the same instrument at a larger scale, and it has one genuinely new property: it is a standing mechanism (常态化合作机制) rather than a signing ceremony.** A standing mechanism can be evaluated over time. **The question is what it will be evaluated on** — and the answer will come from whether the mechanism's own reporting count **projects deployed, developers paid, patches merged**, or **enterprises served, assessments passed, contracts signed.**

**Fifth, the connection to today's first entry, and it is the reason the two belong in one briefing.** CESI's standards working group proposes to **convert active projects into standards**. OpenAtom's intake has just added five more projects and a city through which the conversion can be delivered as a service. **These are not two stories about a conference. They are an input and a process.** The pipeline now has a complete shape: **projects enter the foundation; the foundation and a standards institute convert their practice into standards; a city mechanism delivers certification and assessment against those standards; and procurement consumes the certified result.** Every step is legitimate, every step has a real function, and the whole is a machine for converting **practice into certified compliance**. **What the machine does not do, at any step, is give a contributor who was not on the founding team a decision.** That is not an oversight of this conference. **It is the design of the instrument, and it has been the design of every instrument this series has examined since October.**

**Checkable questions.**

**(i) Will the foundation publish a per-project contribution table for its 64 hosted projects?** This is one page, it can be generated from systems the foundation already runs, and it is the single document that would make every subsequent announcement interpretable.

**(ii) Of the five projects signed on 2026-11-20, which have a public repository, an OSI-approved licence and a published contribution guide?** Five projects signing into a "neutral, open and standardised" incubation system is a claim about governance. The claim is testable within a day.

**(iii) Does the Zhengzhou open-source-city mechanism publish annual outcome metrics, and are they output metrics or service metrics?** "Enterprises served" and "assessments passed" measure the supplier. "Patches merged" and "maintainers outside the donating company" measure the commons.

**(iv) Has any project other than OpenHarmony and openEuler been assessed for graduation in 2026, and against published criteria?** Two graduations in six years, with 64 in the portfolio, is a ratio — and a ratio with an unpublished exit criterion is a hosting service, not an incubator.

### 🔍 Commentary — A Pipeline With a Gate on the Output and None on the Input

**First: today's two stories are one machine, and the machine is the most complete piece of open-source institutional design this ecosystem has assembled.** Trace it end to end. **Input:** five projects enter a ministry-supervised foundation, which absorbs title, licences, legal compliance and CI. **Conversion:** the national electronics standards institute convenes a working group to turn active projects' practice into standards, verifying them in a loop and moving mature ones up through group, industry, national and international rungs. **Delivery:** a city signs a standing mechanism offering training and certification, compatibility assessment and enterprise governance services. **Consumption:** procurement buys against the certified result. **Every node is a real institution doing a real job, and the chain has no gaps.** Compare it to where this series stood six weeks ago, when the finding was that the ecosystem built certifiers, registries and vouchers and had never built the thing that receives a stranger's patch. **The response to that finding has not been to build the missing piece. It has been to build more of the pieces that already existed, around it, until the absence is fully enclosed.** That is a coherent strategy and it should be described as one rather than as a failure.

**Second, the diagnostic that makes the strategy legible: every node in this chain governs an artefact's passage, and none governs the artefact's production.** The foundation governs **who hosts**. The standards group governs **what counts as consensus**. The city mechanism governs **what gets certified and bought**. All three are **downstream of the act that creates the commons**, which is a person deciding to send a change to someone who is not their employer. **This is why the same structural signature keeps appearing in unrelated documents** — a licence that keeps the certification mark while opening the code (OAN, 2026-11-22); a cap table that takes CNY 105.5 billion and confers zero votes (DeepSeek, 2026-11-22); a repository whose entire history is one account editing a README (Hy4, 2026-11-23); a recognition ceremony that thanks 32 developers and grants none of them a merge right (2026-11-24); and today, a standards loop whose input side is "any active project" and whose output side is a committee decision. **Five unrelated institutions, one pattern: intake is generous, the centre does not move, and the thing distributed is participation while the thing retained is the decision.** At this density it is no longer a hypothesis about individual actors. It is a description of an institutional environment.

**Third, the honest counter-argument, which is strong and should be stated before the analysis is mistaken for a verdict.** **China is not alone in putting standards ahead of community, and the order it is using is not irrational.** ISO, IEC, ITU and IEEE have been converting implementation practice into normative text for a century, and every serious open-source project that wants a procurement market ends up in exactly this pipeline. **The Linux Foundation does it; the Apache Software Foundation's projects do it; the CNCF's conformance programme is a certification regime by any other name.** China's version is more administrative, more ministry-adjacent and much more explicit about its upward direction — but the **direction** is not the anomaly. **What is anomalous is the absence of the parallel channel.** In every mature ecosystem there are **two** routes from practice to authority: the **standards route**, through a national body, and the **maintainer route**, through a foundation whose membership is elective, whose technical committees publish their charters, and whose contributors can vote a leadership out. **The first route is centralising by nature and needs the second to check it.** This series has documented the second route's absence repeatedly and has not yet found a single Chinese open community where a contributor not on the founding team holds a binding vote on direction.

**Fourth, the practical consequence for anyone building on Chinese open source, stated plainly because it is the question a real reader has.** **None of today's developments adds a compliance obligation to a foreign adopters' work, and one of them could reduce friction.** If CESI's group publishes converted standards openly, an enterprise adopting a Chinese project gains a **written, citable description of what conformance means** — which is a genuine public good and exactly what a coordination device should be. **The risk is the inverse and it is not hypothetical**: when a national standards body converts a project's practice into a national standard and carries it to ISO or ITU, the standard's text becomes the **authoritative description of the project** in procurement markets the project's own maintainers do not control. **A maintainer who changes the implementation would then be departing from the standard, rather than the standard being an observation of the implementation.** That inversion — where the description outranks the thing described — is how a commons becomes an administered category, and it usually happens without anyone deciding it should. **The single test that prevents it is publication under an open documentation licence with the project's maintainers named as the source of the practice.** That is cheap, it is normal practice, and its presence or absence will settle this question within a release cycle.

**Fifth, what to watch, and every item resolves inside a quarter on the public record.** **A member roster, a chair and a secretariat for the Open Source Standards Working Group** — the three facts that would convert a slogan into an assessable instrument. **The first batch's project list, and whether the maintainers consented** — because the classification is more consequential than the standard. **Publication terms for the converted standards** — open URL and OSI-approved documentation licence, or purchasable text. **A per-project contribution table for OpenAtom's 64 hosted projects** — the one page that would make every intake announcement interpretable. **And: whether any of the five projects signed on 2026-11-20 has, within six months, a merged patch from someone outside the donating company.** One patch. That is the smallest possible evidence that an intake became a commons, and it is the measurement this series will keep applying until one of them comes back positive.

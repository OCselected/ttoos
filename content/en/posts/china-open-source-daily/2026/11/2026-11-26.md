⚠️ **Editorial note: The open source ecosystem in China operates under a distinct institutional framework — characterized by state-led initiatives, intranet-like boundaries, and top-down governance. Readers should be aware that this context differs from the community-driven open source model common in other regions. The term "open source" as used in Chinese media may refer to practices that diverge from the conventional definition.**

## China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-26

### 🏛️ Governance — The Price List Is the Constitution: OpenAtom Publishes Its Donor Ladder

**1. The document, and it is a web page rather than an announcement.** The **OpenAtom Open Source Foundation (开放原子开源基金会)** publishes, on its public cooperation page, a four-tier donation ladder with **minimum annual amounts and, at two of the four tiers, binding multi-year commitments**:

- **Outstanding Donor (杰出捐赠人)** — **no less than CNY 10 million per year**.
- **Platinum Donor (白金捐赠人)** — **no less than CNY 3 million per year**, *and* a commitment to **donate for at least three consecutive years**.
- **Gold Donor (黄金捐赠人)** — **no less than CNY 1 million per year**, *and* a commitment to **donate for at least two consecutive years**.
- **Silver Donor (白银捐赠人)** — **no less than CNY 200,000 per year**.

The same page states what the money buys at the foundation level: *"where the donor does not restrict the funds to a particular activity or project, the donated funds will be centrally arranged by the foundation, used for the maintenance and servicing of open-source project infrastructure, the protection of the OpenAtom trademark, and the support required by various open-source empowerment programmes."* And it publishes the **project-intake procedure** as a five-step sequence: **submit materials → foundation reviews the materials → TOC conducts the incubation review → code compliance detection → both parties sign the donation agreement.** A hosted project's service catalogue is itemised in a table: **funding, fundraising, fund custody, project financial management, CLA/DCO on the Git platform, CI/CD, code compliance scanning, website construction, forums and wikis, BD support and donor exchange.**

**2. The event the ladder was applied at.** On **2026-11-20**, at the opening ceremony of the **2026 OpenAtom Developer Conference** in **Zhengzhou**, the foundation held its **2026 new-donor recognition ceremony (2026年度新增捐赠人致谢仪式)** and presented plaques to **nine new donor organisations — two Platinum, two Gold, five Silver** — in batches by tier:

- **Platinum (2).** **Zhiyuan Innovation (Shanghai) Technology (智元创新)** — which donated the **Lingqu OS (灵渠OS)** project and co-builds the **humanoid robot open-source community**, working on robot bodies and hardware, data and evaluation tools, operating systems, and the "large brain / small brain" architecture. **Beijing Ningsi Software (北京凝思软件)** — a long-standing participant in **openEuler** and foundation ecosystem work.
- **Gold (2).** **Shenzhen Hualong Xunda (深圳华龙讯达)** — building an integrated open-source technology stack for **OpenHarmony in the industrial sector**, aimed at **import substitution in industrial control systems** in key industries. **Shandong Kaihong Digital (山东开鸿数字)** — established under the **Shandong Development Group**, expanding **OpenHarmony industry solutions**.
- **Silver (5).** **Shenzhen Yipu Ecology (亿普生态)** on OpenHarmony hardware adaptation; **Guangdong Zhike Kaihong** on OpenHarmony in **medical and elder care**; **Shanghai Chishun** on OpenHarmony ecosystem development services; **Hengke Technology** on **printer products** built on open compatibility; and the **Global Intelligent IoT Consortium (GIIC)**, founded by IoT and HarmonyOS ecosystem partners.

**3. The cumulative accounting, from the foundation's own June release.** The foundation's **2026-06-25** account of its Beijing ecosystem conference states that since its founding it has had **198 cumulative donors** to the foundation and its projects, spanning *"technology enterprises to financial institutions, state-owned and central enterprises to foreign-invested enterprises"*; that at that June ceremony **48 donor organisations took the stage**; that in 2026 to that date **ten organisations newly joined** — naming **Leju Intelligent (乐聚智能)**, **Loongson Technology (龙芯中科)** and **Sinomach (国机集团)** — while **Shenzhen Kaihong (深开鸿)** and **Tuowei Information (拓维信息)** were **upgraded to Platinum**; and that **twelve donors renewed**. That same release gives the foundation's stated rationale for corporate giving, and it is worth quoting because it is not a commercial rationale: *"the primary driver of enterprise donation is not commercial return, but identification with the cause of open-source public welfare — advancing technological equality (科技平权), so that advanced technology is no longer monopolised by a few vendors and SMEs and individual developers can obtain it on equal terms."*

**4. What the foundation says the money becomes.** Four core functions are named: **project incubation** (project governance, community building, brand endorsement); **talent cultivation** (the **校源行** campus programme, open-source competitions, the developer conference, and **open-source talent evaluation and certification**); **enterprise enablement** (neutral governance and open collaboration); and **ecosystem connection**. The release also states the downstream commercial results it attributes to participation: **OpenHarmony ecosystem partners have shipped commercial distributions — 电鸿 (power), 矿鸿 (mining), 城轨鸿 (urban rail), 警鸿 (policing)** — for power, mining, transport and public security; **Leju Intelligent leads the OpenLET embodied-intelligence open-dataset community**; **China Southern Power Grid built 电鸿, the country's first unified IoT system for the power industry**; and **Runkaihong (润开鸿) received strategic investment from a national-level industrial investment fund** and reports scaled commercial deployment in finance, power, medical, education and smart cities.

**Institutional Analysis & Commentary:**

**This is the most legible document this series has found in the Chinese open-source ecosystem all year, and the reason is not that it says anything surprising. It is that it publishes the price.**

**First, credit the transparency honestly, because on the standard this series uses it passes.** Almost every important fact about Chinese open-source foundations is unavailable: per-project contribution tables, membership rosters, graduation criteria, working-group composition. **OpenAtom has now published the one thing foundations elsewhere often keep quiet — exactly what each tier of support costs and what the minimum commitment is.** Platinum requires **three consecutive years at CNY 3 million or more**; Gold requires **two years at CNY 1 million or more**. A **multi-year minimum commitment** is a genuinely constraining term, and it is the kind of term that tells you the foundation is trying to solve a real problem: **a commons needs predictable funding more than it needs generosity**, and a single large one-off gift is worth less to an institution than a smaller annuity. That is a competent piece of institutional design, and it should be said plainly before anything else is said.

**Second, and this is the structural observation: the ladder is the only constitution this ecosystem has published, and it governs the wrong side of the transaction.** Read what each tier buys. **Platinum gives you a seat at the highest recognition level and, in practice, the projects you donate arrive with their own donor tier attached.** Zhiyuan Innovation gives **Lingqu OS** at Platinum; Shandong Kaihong, established by a provincial state-owned development group, gives at Gold. **What no tier buys — and what no published document grants at any price — is a vote.** There is no membership class in the ladder. There is no tier that confers a technical-committee seat, a maintainer right, or an electorate. **The 198 donors, the 48 honoured in Beijing, the 9 honoured in Zhengzhou: all of them are on the funding side of a boundary whose other side is the TOC, and the TOC's composition is not on this page.** The price list is precise about money and silent about power, and in **Hirschman's** terms this is exit without voice: **you may leave, you may pay, you may not speak** — the ladder is the schedule of prices at which you are thanked.

**Third, the economics, stated properly, and it is not a scandal.** A foundation that holds assets shared by competitors is solving a **hold-up problem**: each member would like the others to fund the shared infrastructure and would rather not fund it themselves. **Olson's** logic of collective action predicts under-provision, and the standard remedies are **selective incentives** — something valuable available only to those who pay. A **donor tier with a plaque, a logo right and a named relationship** is exactly that selective incentive, and it is how every foundation in the world raises money; the Linux Foundation publishes its membership tiers in the same form. **The interesting question is not whether OpenAtom prices participation. It is what this foundation's revenue structure does to its selection of projects.** If the median donor is a **state-owned or state-adjacent enterprise giving at Silver or Gold to be associated with OpenHarmony distributions sold into procurement**, then the projects that attract donors are the ones **with procurement markets**, not the ones with contributors — and the foundation's own release confirms the pattern without being asked to: **every single named commercial outcome in the June document is an industry distribution of OpenHarmony.** 电鸿, 矿鸿, 城轨鸿, 警鸿. Power, mining, rail, police. **Four sectors, four buyers, one upstream project.** The portfolio this revenue structure selects for is the portfolio of **things a state-owned buyer can purchase**, and this series measured that shape independently on **2026-11-25** when it found that **3 of 64 projects have graduated and 10 have a working committee.**

**Fourth, the sentence that deserves the most scrutiny is the one about motive.** *"The primary driver of enterprise donation is not commercial return, but identification with the cause of open-source public welfare."* **It may well be true of the individuals who sign the cheques.** But note what the very next paragraph does: it lists, as the results of donation, that **Runkaihong received strategic investment from a national-level industrial fund** and that **China Southern Power Grid occupies the first-mover position in its industry's digital transformation.** A foundation that says the motive is not commercial, and then evidences its success with **commercial outcomes including a national fund's investment in a member firm**, is describing a **reciprocity structure** rather than denying one. **This is not hypocrisy; it is the ordinary ambiguity of a public-benefit institution whose members are its beneficiaries**, and it is precisely why the boundary between *donation* and *investment* has to be published rather than asserted. The ladder publishes the donation. Nothing publishes the investment.

**Fifth, the academic framing that fits, and it makes the design legible.** **Ostrom's** commons design principles begin with **clearly defined boundaries** — who is in, who is out — and with **collective-choice arrangements**, the rule that those affected by a set of rules can participate in modifying them. OpenAtom's published material establishes the **first** with unusual clarity: there is an intake procedure, a review body, a compliance check, a signed agreement, and a priced donor ladder. **It does not establish the second anywhere this series can find.** The **TOC** is named once, as the body that runs the incubation review; its membership, its appointment method and its accountability are not published on the pages examined. **A commons with a boundary rule and no collective-choice rule is not a commons in Ostrom's sense — it is a trust.** A trust is a perfectly respectable institution. It is governed by trustees rather than by participants, and the correct question about a trust is not "is it democratic" but **"to whom do the trustees answer, and what happens when they answer badly?"** For OpenAtom the answer on the public record is: **the supervisory ministry and the annual inspection.** That is a real accountability mechanism, and it is an accountability to the state, not to the contributors.

**Sixth, the observation that connects this to the rest of the week, and it is the reason this belongs in the series rather than in a funding-round digest.** On **2026-11-25** this briefing described a pipeline — **projects enter the foundation, a standards institute converts their practice into standards, a city mechanism certifies and delivers, procurement consumes** — and asked what the machine does not do. Today's document answers it from the revenue side. **The machine's fuel is donor money, its fuel is priced by tier, and the tiers correlate with procurement.** The CESI standards group exists to convert projects into industry-consensus standards; the Zhengzhou mechanism exists to deliver certification and assessment services; the donors at Platinum and Gold are the vendors whose distributions will be assessed against those standards. **Every node is separately legitimate and the whole is a closed loop in which the party that funds the institution, the party whose project is standardised, and the party that sells the certified product can be the same company — and nothing in the published record says they must not be.** That is not corruption. It is **vertical integration of the governance chain**, and the reason it matters is the same reason it matters in any market: **the party setting the standard and the party being certified against it should be distinguishable by a reader.**

**Checkable questions.**

**(i) Does the foundation publish the TOC's membership, the method of appointment, and the term of office?** The intake procedure names the TOC as the deciding body. A body that decides which projects enter the commons, and whose members are unnamed, is the single largest gap left in an otherwise well-published design.

**(ii) Is there any membership class in which an individual contributor — not a company — holds a vote?** The donor ladder prices organisations. If no corresponding contributor membership exists, the foundation is a funding trust with a project portfolio, and should be described that way rather than as a neutral community foundation.

**(iii) What share of the 198 cumulative donors are state-owned or state-controlled enterprises, and what share of total revenue do the top ten donors provide?** Two numbers. They would tell an outside reader whether this is a broad-based public-benefit institution or a concentrated consortium with a public-benefit charter.

**(iv) Will the foundation publish, per project, the donor tier attached to it at intake — and the proportion of that project's contributors employed by that donor?** This is the measurement that converts today's ladder from a funding schedule into a governance document, and the data already exists inside the foundation.

### 🏗️ Institutional Change — A Conference With No Ministry in the Title: COSCon'26 and the Municipal Absorption

**1. The organiser line, read slowly, because the organiser line is the finding.** The **eleventh China Open Source Conference (COSCon'26)** ran **2026-11-14 and 15** at the **Yungu Centre (云谷中心)** in **Xihu district, Hangzhou**, on the theme *"Open Source: The Next Generation" (开源下一代)*. It was:

- **guided (指导) by** the **Hangzhou Municipal AI Open Source Ecology Academy (杭州市人工智能开源生态学院)**;
- **organised (主办) by** **KAIYUANSHE (开源社)** and **Datawhale**;
- **co-organised (联合主办) by** **Chaoxinwen (潮新闻)**, **Chuanbo Danao (传播大脑)** and the **Yungu Centre**;
- **supported (支持) by** the **Hangzhou Open Source AI Foundation (杭州市开源人工智能基金会)**, the **Xihu district party committee talent office (西湖区委人才办)**, the **Hangzhou Zijin'gang Science and Technology City Management Committee**, and the **Zhejiang AI Academy**.

**No ministry. No national foundation. No national standards institute.** The convening body is a **community organisation founded in 2015** that has now held this event eleven times, and its own description of itself is *"a unique neutral community positioning"* and *"cross-organisational, cross-project broad coverage"*. **First-day attendance exceeded 1,000.** COSCon has been held in Hangzhou for the first time in eleven years.

**2. The community's own framing, in the words of its chair.** **Lin Lüqiang (林旅强)**, co-founder and 2026 chair of KAIYUANSHE and vice-president of ecosystem at Datastrato: *"Open source is especially important in the AI revolution, so as long as we are willing to take the next step, no matter how old we are, no matter where we are today, we are all 'the next generation of open source'."* The association's stated vision: *"based in China, contributing to the world, making open source a way of life in the new era."* KAIYUANSHE's self-described work covers **open-source governance, international alignment, community development, project incubation, public-benefit work and talent.**

**3. The city's framing, in the words of the co-chair, and the two framings are not the same framing.** **Fan Jingjing (范晶晶)**, founder of **Datawhale** — described as *"the largest AI open-source learning community in the country, having served over 2 million learners across more than 4,000 universities at home and abroad"* — put it this way: *"Today in China, no city can promote AI and the open-source ecosystem as a core strategy the way Hangzhou does. The national 15th Five-Year Plan has already listed the open-source ecosystem as a key path to building an AI innovation highland; Hangzhou wants to build the number-one city for AI innovation and development, the open-source ecosystem is one of the three special actions, and Xihu district is the main battlefield for that action."*

**4. The instrument that makes the absorption concrete, and it is a talent standard.** In **September 2026** the **first AI open-source ecology academy in the country** — the **Hangzhou Municipal AI Open Source Ecology Academy** — was established at the Yungu Centre, jointly with **Hangzhou Dianzi University** and the Xihu district government. Its founding dean is **Xu Xuchu (徐旭初)**, a professor at Hangzhou Dianzi University. The academy published the **OPC nine-level talent competency standard (OPC九级人才能力标准, trial)** — a nine-level progression framework spanning AI and adjacent disciplines — and the academy's model is described as *"a school without walls"*, on the principle that *"the ecosystem comes first, then the students"*, with real projects as the classroom and mentors drawn from universities, communities and industry. At COSCon the city named a first cohort of **"open-source mentors" (开源导师)** — presented by **Zhang Xiaojun** of the Chengxi Sci-Tech Innovation Corridor's investment and industry development bureau and **Xu Dongxu** of the Zijin'gang Science and Technology City party working committee and management committee. The **Hangzhou AI + OPC entrepreneurship policy measures** were cited as the policy backing.

**5. And the sentence that states the mechanism better than any analysis could.** In the interview after the main forum, **Angie Jones** (vice-president of the Agentic AI Foundation), **Lin Lüqiang**, **Li Mingkang** (KAIYUANSHE director and executive, Qiniu Cloud's open-source ecosystem lead) and **Si Yuxin** (Datawhale ecosystem) converged on this judgement, reported in the coverage: *"The three highlands Xihu district is tasked with building — an intelligent talent cultivation highland, an open-source model ecosystem highland, and a scenario-innovation application highland — give the open-source community an institutional reception space (制度性承接空间) in Xihu district; and the Hangzhou AI+OPC policy and the academy's nine-level standard thread learning, contribution, recognition and scenario reception into one pathway, giving the contribution value and hands-on capability inside the open-source community, for the first time, a scale legible to public policy (第一次有了可被公共政策识别的刻度)."*

**6. The other thing that happened, because it is the story a Chinese open-source reader would notice first.** The programme also released the **China AI Open Source Ecology Blue Paper (中国人工智能开源生态蓝皮书)** in Chinese and English, with Xu Xuchu presenting its structure around five questions — *what it is, why it exists, what the current state is, where the problems lie, how to advance* — and its conclusion that China's AI open-source ecosystem shows *"application-pull plus multi-party coordination"* with head enterprises, local government, universities and communities all playing major roles, while still facing key bottlenecks. Its stated aim: *"to move China from participant toward ecosystem builder and rule co-builder in the global AI competition."* International speakers included **Angie Jones**, **Kevin Xu** of GitHub (on the economic impact of open source), **Silvano Luciani** of Kong, **Mario Behling** of FOSSASIA and **Richard Littauer** of CURIOSS and SustainOSS. Some two hundred speakers appeared across tracks including **industrial open-source AI, embodied-intelligence open source, AI-era open-source talent, next-generation open-source education and women in open source** — and a session on **next-generation open-source people** featuring **high-school students** from Shenzhen, Qinglan Shan and Yungu schools.

**Institutional Analysis & Commentary:**

**This is the first time in this series' coverage that a genuine community institution, one that predates the ministry foundations and answers to no ministry, has been documented doing the thing community institutions are supposed to do — and being absorbed by a city government in the act of doing it. Both halves of that sentence are the finding.**

**First, state the difference from OpenAtom plainly, because the contrast is the whole analytical content of today's briefing.** OpenAtom was founded in **June 2020**, supervises under MIIT, files statutory annual inspections, prices donations in millions, and convenes its conference with ministry officials opening it. KAIYUANSHE was **founded by the community in 2015**; COSCon is its event, its chair is an ecosystem vice-president at a startup, its programme is curated by volunteers, and its most senior state presence is a **district-level talent office and a science-city management committee**. **One institution was built by the state to host projects. The other was built by practitioners to hold a conference.** *That two such different bodies coexist in the same ecosystem, in the same month, both claiming the word "open source", and neither describing the other, is the most informative structural fact published this week.*

**Second, the honest and rather admiring point about KAIYUANSHE's persistence.** Eleven annual conferences since 2015 through every shift in this ecosystem's politics, with a **deliberately neutral positioning** and a **cross-organisational, cross-project** remit, is real institutional endurance of the kind that is hard to fake. **A conference is a weak institution** — it produces no artefacts, holds no assets, and cannot compel anyone. **But it is also the only place in this ecosystem where a maintainer from a ministry foundation, a founder from a private lab, an Apache member, a foreign foundation vice-president and a high-school contributor sit in the same room as equals**, and there is nothing else in the Chinese open-source institutional landscape that does that. **In Ostrom's terms the conference is a boundary-crossing institution** — it manufactures a common space precisely where the formal institutions have drawn boundaries. **That is a genuine public good, weakly funded and annually renewed, which is exactly how a public good looks before someone institutionalises it.**

**Third, the absorption, described precisely, because it is not capture and the vocabulary matters.** The city did not take over COSCon. It did something subtler and, from the community's perspective, more attractive: **it built an institution to receive what COSCon produces.** The academy in September, the nine-level talent standard, the open-source mentor certificates handed out on stage, the AI+OPC entrepreneurship policy, the three "highlands" assigned to Xihu district — these are **reception structures (承接空间)**, and the community's own representatives used that exact word. **What flows in is the thing this series has been looking for in every other Chinese open-source institution and not finding: a route by which contribution converts into recognised standing.** A student who contributes to an open-source project gets, under the OPC standard, a **level**; that level is **legible to public policy**; public policy funds talent programmes, incubators and scenario access against it. **For the first time in this ecosystem, the sequence contribution → recognition → resource has an institutional path, and it runs through a municipal government rather than through a foundation, a standards institute or a procurement office.**

**Fourth, and here is the cost, stated without alarmism because the participants are not naive about it.** **Anything a government can recognise, a government can define.** The moment contribution becomes a **level on a nine-point public scale**, three things follow mechanically. **One: the scale will be gamed**, because a measurable contribution metric is an incentive, and the fastest way to move up a recognised scale is to optimise for what the scale measures — commits, stars, certificates, course completion — none of which is the same thing as a merged patch from a stranger to a project that mattered. This series has documented what optimising for recognised metrics does to an ecosystem: the **star-to-watch ratio** pattern it examined on **2026-10-26**, and the **2,600 certified compatible products** alongside **110,000 certificates issued** it examined on **2026-11-24**. **Two: the academy will define what counts**, because a nine-level standard authored by a college's founding dean and a district's talent office is a public curriculum, and a public curriculum is a definition of the field. **Three: the definition will bind hardest on the people least able to route around it** — students, who need the certificate for the job, and SME engineers, whose employers will check the level. **A senior maintainer with fifteen years of merges does not need a level. A graduate does. And a level that a graduate needs and a maintainer ignores will end up describing the graduate's labour market, not the commons.**

**Fifth, the mechanism in the terms that fit, and it is the same mechanism this series named in Xi'an and Zhengzhou, arriving by a different door.** In **Scott's** vocabulary this is **legibility**: a state — here a municipal one — making a practice readable so it can be administered, funded and steered. In **Hayek's**, the voluntary collaboration that produced the contribution is **tacit, distributed and known to the participants**, and a **nine-level standard is a centralisation of that knowledge in a form the centre can use**. **Neither of those observations is an accusation.** Cities fund schools and cities define curricula everywhere; no serious person thinks a municipal talent programme destroys the discipline it is trying to cultivate. **What is specific here, and what makes this worth a briefing rather than a paragraph, is the direction of the flow.** In the pipeline this series described yesterday, **the community's output flowed up into standards and the community's members were the raw material.** Here the flow is different and better: **the community is the author of the standard and the city is the adopter.** Xu Xuchu, the dean, came out of the community's own orbit; the mentors were named *by* the conference; the standard was released *at* COSCon. **That is the right order. It is also the fragile order, because the second year of a municipal programme is written by the city, not by the conference.**

**Sixth, the uncomfortable observation that this series has to make because it has made it nine times this month.** **The Chinese open-source ecosystem now has two institutional paths and they are not talking to each other.** **Path A** — the ministry foundation, the standards institute, the city procurement mechanism — **prices participation, publishes the price, converts projects into standards and delivers certification as a service, and grants no contributor a vote.** **Path B** — the community conference, the community academy, the municipal talent standard — **converts contribution into recognised standing, grants the community authorship of the standard, and makes no claim on any project's governance at all.** Both paths are legitimate. Neither path, as published, contains the institution this series has been looking for since October: **an open-source community in China where a contributor who was not on the founding team holds a binding vote on the project's direction.** Path A will not build it because it does not need it. Path B cannot build it because a conference and an academy do not govern repositories. **What would build it is the one thing neither path has announced: a project charter, published, with an electorate.** The likeliest candidate is not a ministry and not a city — it is a **company that has already learned it needs outside maintainers**, and the observable test remains the same one this series has used all month: **a merged patch from someone outside the founding company.**

**Checkable questions.**

**(i) Will the OPC nine-level talent standard be published in full, with the evidence required at each level, and who sits on the body that awards levels?** A competency standard is a labour-market institution. Its text and its awarding body are the two facts that determine whether it measures capability or attendance.

**(ii) Does the standard assign *any* weight to a merged contribution to a project the candidate did not start — and can that be verified from a public forge?** This is the difference between a standard that rewards participation in open source and a standard that rewards open source.

**(iii) Will KAIYUANSHE's own governance be published — board elections, membership, financials — at the level now routinely published by the foundation it does not belong to?** A community institution claiming a neutral position is a stronger claim than a ministry foundation makes, and it deserves the same scrutiny rather than less.

**(iv) When the city writes year two of the programme, who drafts the standard — the academy's community-appointed dean, or the district's talent office?** The answer will be visible in the authorship line of the revised document, and it settles whether COSCon's absorption was a genuine reception or the beginning of an annexation.

### 🔍 Commentary — Two Cities, Two Recognisers, and the Question Neither Asks

**First: read the week as a controlled comparison, because that is what it accidentally is.** Within seven days, two Chinese institutions each held a ceremony honouring people and organisations with plaques. **Zhengzhou, 2026-11-20:** a ministry-supervised foundation thanked **32 developers** selected on twelve-month contribution records by a **quota formula**, and **nine new donors** by **price tier** — Platinum from CNY 3 million a year for three years, Silver at CNY 200,000. **Hangzhou, 2026-11-14:** a community association and an AI learning community, at their eleventh annual conference, named **open-source mentors**, launched a **nine-level talent standard**, and released a **blue paper** — and a district government put its talent office on the supporter line. **Both ceremonies are recognition systems. They recognise different things, they are funded differently, and they answer to different principals.** Set them side by side and the ecosystem's whole institutional architecture becomes visible in a way that neither document shows alone.

**Second, the comparison that makes the price ladder meaningful: what is being priced.** OpenAtom prices **organisations**, by the year, with multi-year commitments, and the currency is money. The Hangzhou academy prices **individuals**, by level, in a nine-point scale, and the currency is **legibility** — a level a policy can read. **A foundation sells standing to a company; an academy certifies capability in a person.** Those are not competing products; they are the two halves of what any ecosystem needs to allocate, and the interesting question is which half each institution can actually deliver. OpenAtom can deliver **funding stability** and **a legal home** and it cannot deliver **a vote**. The academy can deliver **a credential** and it cannot deliver **a maintainership**. **Neither can deliver the one thing that makes an open-source project a commons: the right of a stranger who has done the work to decide what merges.**

**Third, the sentence from the Hangzhou coverage that is the most important sentence published in Chinese open source this week, and it was said by the participants rather than by an analyst.** *"Contribution value and hands-on capability inside the open-source community have, for the first time, a scale legible to public policy."* **Read what that claims and what it concedes.** It claims a genuine breakthrough: before this, contribution in China had **no recognised form** — you could not put five years of merges on a résumé that a state talent programme would read. **That is not a small grievance.** This series has catalogued it repeatedly as the incommensurability between the open-source world's currency and the administrative world's: the person who has done the work is invisible to the institution that allocates the resources. **Hangzhou has built an exchange rate.** That is a real institutional innovation and it deserves to be named as one. **And it concedes, in the word "legible", exactly what it costs**: legibility is not the same as validity. **A thing becomes legible by being simplified into categories, and categories are authored by whoever holds the pen.** The community got to hold the pen for year one.

**Fourth, the comparison to where the rest of the ecosystem sits, because it reframes a criticism this series has made for two months.** The criticism has been that the Chinese open-source ecosystem builds **instruments that govern artefacts** — licences, standards, certifications, export filings, recognition ceremonies — **and never builds the instrument that governs a commons**, which is a membership with a vote. **Hangzhou has now demonstrated that the missing instrument can be built, cheaply, and that what is hard is not the design but the demand.** The nine-level standard is not a governance instrument; it is a labour-market instrument. **But it exists, it was built by practitioners, and it was adopted in nine weeks.** Meanwhile, no Chinese open community in this series' entire record has published a charter with an electorate — **and the reason is not that it is technically difficult. It is that nobody with the authority to publish one has an incentive to.**

**Fifth, what an outside reader should take from this, stated plainly because the practical question is always the same.** **For a foreign enterprise or maintainer looking at Chinese open source:** the Hangzhou model is the more hopeful signal of the two, because it shows a **local state willing to fund recognition of contribution rather than ownership of artefacts** — and if the nine-level standard stays a talent credential rather than becoming a procurement requirement, it costs an outside contributor nothing. **The thing to watch is not the standard. It is whether the standard starts appearing in government procurement documents**, at which point a credential becomes a licence condition — the exact transition this series documented for the **identity code** on **2026-11-07** and for the **secure-and-reliable assessment** on **2026-11-21**. **KAIYUANSHE's neutrality is genuine and worth defending; a municipal adopter is the fastest route to a public good and the fastest route to a gate.** Both are true at once, and the way to keep the first is to publish the boundary.

**Sixth, what to watch, all of it checkable inside two quarters.** **The full text of the OPC nine-level standard, with its awarding body named** — a credential without a published awarding procedure is a certificate with a logo. **Whether KAIYUANSHE publishes its own charter, board elections and financials** — the community institution should hold itself to the standard it implicitly applies to others. **Whether any Chinese open-source project publishes a charter with an electorate in 2027**, and the observable proxy remains **a merged patch from a maintainer outside the founding company.** **And the number this series will keep asking for and has never received: OpenAtom's per-project contribution table for its 64 projects.** One page. It would make every ceremony in this week's comparison interpretable — the plaques, the prices, the levels and the blue paper would all resolve into a single question: **were the people being thanked the people doing the work?** That question is not rhetorical. It has an answer, it is in a database, and no institution in this briefing has published it.

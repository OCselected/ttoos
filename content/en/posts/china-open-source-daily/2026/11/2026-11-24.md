⚠️ **Editorial note: The open source ecosystem in China operates under a distinct institutional framework — characterized by state-led initiatives, intranet-like boundaries, and top-down governance. Readers should be aware that this context differs from the community-driven open source model common in other regions. The term "open source" as used in Chinese media may refer to practices that diverge from the conventional definition.**

## China Open Source Daily — 2026-11-24

### ⚖️ Legal & Licensing — Publication Is Now an Export: What Announcement No. 48 Does to the Open-Source Release

**1. The document, and the one clause in it that is doing all the work.** On **2026-10-16**, the **Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM)** and the **Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST)** issued **Joint Announcement No. 48 [2026]**, opening public comment — **16 October to 15 November 2026** — on a revision to the **Catalogue of Technologies Prohibited or Restricted from Export (中国禁止出口限制出口技术目录)**. The revision proposes **40 technologies across three fields**: **23 in artificial intelligence**, **5 in biomedicine**, **12 in photovoltaics**. Of the 40, **14 would be prohibited** from export and **26 restricted**. In the AI field alone the draft reaches **large models, agents, computing clusters and information processing**.

The clause that matters is in the policy explanation, and it is not in the catalogue itself. It reads: **"technologies that have already entered the public domain by means of open source or academic publication are not included in the scope of management" (对已通过开源、发表学术论文等方式进入公共领域的技术，不纳入管理范围)** — and, separately, that for technical export involving **AI open source, overseas self-built and self-operated computing centres, overseas chip tape-out, joint R&D using overseas resources, and participation in international standard-setting**, the authorities will apply **automatic licensing (自动许可) and other facilitation measures, essentially achieving "report and out" (即报即出)**.

**2. The interpretation nobody in the coverage disputed, and it is the whole story.** **Liu Xinyu (刘新宇)** — managing partner of King & Wood Mallesons' cross-border regulatory team and co-director of Renmin University's Institute of Customs and Foreign Exchange Law — stated the consequence plainly: **"previously the compliance boundary for open-sourcing technology was rather vague; this revision explicitly recognises 'open source' as a form of technology export, that is, open-sourcing controlled technology to overseas parties is equivalent to technology export" (本次修订明确将"开源"认定为技术出口的一种形式，即向境外"开源"受控技术等同于技术出口)**. He then drew the two-sided conclusion: **what falls in the restricted class still requires the operator to perform declaration procedures and obtain a licence before it may be open-sourced lawfully; what touches the prohibited red line may not be released abroad in open-source form at all.**

**3. The registration gate opened immediately, ahead of the final rule.** The announcement did not wait for consultation to close. From **16 October** until the revised catalogue formally takes effect, exporters of any listed technology must **register** on MOFCOM's unified platform. The registration reaches four transaction types: **transfer of technology ownership abroad (e.g. IP assignment)**; **domestic technology licensed for foreign use (e.g. IP licensing)**; **change of a domestic technology owner or actual controller to a foreign entity**; and **other circumstances MOFCOM specifies by notice**. It also covers **exports already begun but not completed**. The information required: exporter and recipient identities, destination country or region, the technology and its intended use, and the estimated export value.

**4. Eight days later, two firms walked through it, and the reporting framed it as good news.** On **2026-10-23**, **MiniMax** — a Hong Kong-listed company — **announced to the market** that it had **completed the MOFCOM technology-export registration required for the open-source release and overseas authorised use of several recently released and expected-to-be-released models**; its shares closed **up more than 16 per cent** that day. The same day, CCTV's new-media account **Yuyuantantian (玉渊谭天)** disclosed that **DeepSeek** had likewise completed registration and **"walked through the compliance process for the open-source release and overseas authorisation of its large models"**. The trade press's reading, from a brokerage computer-sector chief analyst quoted by **STAR Market Daily**: *"the completion of registration by these two leading model vendors means that, alongside strict control of core sensitive technologies, the pathway abroad for general-purpose open-source large models has instead become clearer."* A founder of an overseas-facing B2B AI startup added the practitioner's version: *"previously we could only judge the compliance line ourselves; to avoid risk, a lot of overseas business was advanced conservatively."*

**5. What is in each class, because the classes are the policy.** Per the MOFCOM question-and-answer and the firm-level analyses: **proposed prohibitions** include **AI-driven malicious code generation and mutation**, and — critically — **large models trained on core data and used to provide services (涉及核心数据训练的大模型拟禁止出口)**. **Proposed restrictions** include **models trained on important data**; **core technologies of large models exceeding specified capability thresholds — weights, key checkpoints, source code, architecture, and training and inference optimisation**; **advanced agent technologies meeting performance thresholds** (task planning, multi-agent collaboration, tool calling, game decision-making); **AI processor and memory chip design**; **high-speed interconnect, fault tolerance, communication scheduling and heterogeneous scheduling for computing clusters**; and **AI-assisted vulnerability discovery**.

**The service distinction is what keeps the ecosystem running, and MOFCOM stated it explicitly.** Whether an overseas activity counts as technology export turns on **whether restricted technology moves from inside China to outside it**. **Overseas localised deployment of a restricted model is a technology export**; deployment on an overseas self-owned cloud, being lower-risk, may take **automatic licensing**. **API access, app or web interaction, SaaS applications, and lightweight on-device deployment are outside the scope entirely.** API calls are not exports. The checkpoint file is.

**Institutional Analysis & Commentary:**

**This is the single most consequential institutional document for Chinese open source since the Mulan licence was donated, and its importance is that it does not argue about what open source is. It assigns open source a legal location, and the location is the export-control regime.**

**First, credit the drafting, because a careless reading will call this a crackdown and it is not one.** The instrument is **precise, tiered and deliberately narrow**. Most listed technologies carry **specific technical indicators**; **products made with the technology are out of scope**; commentary is open for a month; **obsolete technologies are to be removed on a rolling basis**; and MOFCOM's answer spends conspicuously more words on **what is not covered** than on what is. The facilitation list is real and tailored to the ecosystem's actual bottlenecks: **open source, overseas self-built compute, overseas tape-out, joint R&D, standards participation** all take **automatic licensing**. And the bedrock concession is one most export-control regimes do not make: **anything already public through open-source release or academic publication is out of scope.** A model already on Hugging Face stays free. The regime is prospective, not retrospective.

**Second, the diagnosis, and it is a matter of legal form rather than intent. Open source has been located inside a licensing regime, and that changes what a publication *is*.** In the ordinary understanding this series has been using all year, an open-source release is the **opposite** of an export: it is the act of **relinquishing** the ability to control distribution, which is why it is institutionally interesting and why it is a commons. Announcement No. 48 assigns it a different legal character: **open-sourcing a controlled technology to an overseas party is a transfer of the technology across the border, and therefore an export like any other.** The code is not being restricted in what it does. It is being reclassified in what it is. **A release that must be licensed before it happens is not a commons being created; it is a permit being exercised** — and the distinction survives every reassurance about automatic approval, because **the obligation to file is the substance and the speed of approval is the courtesy.**

**Third, the institutional economics, stated in the terms that fit.** This is, textbook, a **reassignment of a residual right of control**. Under a permissive licence the licensor has already surrendered the right to decide who uses the artefact downstream; what the state has now done is **restore a decision point at the moment of publication, upstream of the licence**, and place it with a ministry. In **Coase's** framing, rights must be well-defined before they can be exchanged, and the question is never whether rights exist but **who holds them and at what cost they may be moved**. Here the cost of moving an artefact across the border has gone from **zero** (the marginal cost of a `git push`, which is exactly what made open source institutionally distinctive) to **the cost of a filing, a waiting period, and a discretionary review whose criteria for the restricted class are stated in capability thresholds rather than in a published decision procedure.** In **Williamson's** L2/L3 vocabulary, this is a shift in the **institutional environment** that immediately regenerates the **governance structures** beneath it: every Chinese lab now needs an export-compliance function it did not have in September, and the compliance function sits between the researcher and the repository.

**Fourth, the two-sided effect on the commons, because it cuts both ways and honest analysis must hold both.** **The encouragement is real.** "Open source" now appears in a state document as a **favoured** category, one entitled to the fastest available approval channel. Compare it to overseas localised deployment, which requires a licence on ordinary terms. **The regime is explicitly telling firms that publishing weights is the low-friction path and shipping the model to a customer's data centre is the high-friction one.** For a lab deciding between open and closed, that is a thumb on the scale for open. **And the constraint is equally real, and it lands hardest on the community rather than the firm.** A frontier lab has counsel, a filing officer and a relationship with the regulator. **A university group, an individual maintainer, a startup with three engineers do not** — and the compliance question they now face ("does my model's capability exceed the threshold, and is my training data 'important data'?") is one they **cannot answer from the published text**, because the thresholds are in an annexe of indicators and "core data" and "important data" are defined in a different statute entirely. **When compliance is cheap for incumbents and expensive for newcomers, the open-source ecosystem's entry dynamics change even where no release is refused.** The gate that matters is not the one that closes; it is the one that costs nothing to walk through if you have lawyers.

**Fifth, and this is the finding that makes the day's two documents a pair rather than two stories: the state is writing open source into two regulatory regimes that disagree about what publication does.** On **2026-09-07**, the **Supreme People's Court** issued its **Opinions on the Adjudication of AI-Related Disputes (Fa Fa [2026] No. 10)** — 24 articles in five sections. Its **Article XIII** addresses tort liability for open-source software used in AI development, and takes a **lenient** line: courts must weigh **the type of open-source licence, the specific restrictions on rights, the security compliance measures taken, and the degree of information disclosure** — and where a developer or provider **supplies free open-source code modules for AI R&D and publicly explains their functions and security risks**, the court **may find that developer or provider not liable** for torts committed by third parties using those modules downstream.

**Set the two side by side.** The **export regime** says: **an open-source release of a controlled technology is a transfer across the border, and a controlled act.** The **judicial opinion** says: **a free open-source release with public disclosure of functions and risks is the basis for exempting the releaser from liability for what others do with it.** One instrument treats publication as the moment **control is exercised**; the other treats publication as the moment **responsibility is discharged**. **Both are coherent. They are not obviously compatible, and nobody has yet had to reconcile them** — because no case has arisen in which a firm published weights without a licence and a downstream party caused harm.

**Sixth, the arithmetic that should be stated for what it is.** The concession — *"already public technologies are not included"* — is generous and it is also **a grandfathering clause with an expiry that nobody has published**. **Every model released before the final catalogue takes effect is exempt forever. Every model released after it is subject to a filing.** The incentive this creates is unambiguous and it is not the one anyone intended: **there is now a reason to release frontier models early**, before the catalogue lands, and a reason to describe releases as research publications. Whether that becomes a race is an empirical question this series will be able to answer, and answer soon. **It is also the clearest example yet of a general property of Chinese open-source policy that this series has documented all year: the instrument is directional in intent and indeterminate in effect, because its consequences run through the timing of events nobody is coordinating.**

**Checkable questions.**

**(i) Will the final catalogue publish the capability thresholds in a form a third party can apply to their own model without asking MOFCOM?** Automatic licensing is a courtesy only if the licensee can determine that they qualify. A threshold in an annexe plus a private enquiry is a discretionary permit wearing the label of an automatic one.

**(ii) Does "core data" and "important data" in the AI entries refer to the definitions in the Data Security Law and its implementing rules, or to a separate standard?** The prohibition on exporting models trained on core data is the heaviest item in the package, and the class is imported from another regime without the mapping being restated.

**(iii) How many firms file, and can any of them be small?** Two top-tier labs in eight days is a proof of concept, not a distribution. The metric that matters for an open ecosystem is **whether a university group or a three-person startup completes registration** — the public record will show whether the path is walkable or merely walkable by those with counsel.

**(iv) Will the first post-catalogue release by any major Chinese lab arrive before or after the final rule takes effect?** A cluster of releases in the consultation window would be evidence that the grandfathering clause is being read as a deadline, and that would be a finding about incentives rather than about policy.

### 🏛️ Policy & Regulation — The Court Inverts the Closed Box: Article XIII and the Liability Price of Publishing

**1. The document.** On **2026-09-07**, the **Supreme People's Court (最高人民法院)** issued the **Opinions on the Adjudication of AI-Related Disputes in Accordance with the Law (《关于依法审理涉人工智能纠纷案件的意见》, Fa Fa [2026] No. 10)** — **24 articles in five sections**, and the first judicial policy document from the PRC's highest court devoted to AI disputes. It does not create an AI statute. It tells lower courts **how to apply existing law** — the **Civil Code, Civil Procedure Law, Cybersecurity Law, Data Security Law, Copyright Law, Patent Law, Anti-Unfair Competition Law, Consumer Rights Protection Law, Road Traffic Safety Law, Personal Information Protection Law** — to a class of cases that did not previously have a framework.

**2. Article XIII, which is the article this series has been waiting for.** Under the heading of open-source software liability, the Opinions direct courts adjudicating cases involving open-source software used in AI development to consider **four factors**: **the type of open-source licence; the specific restrictions on rights; the security and compliance measures adopted; and the degree of information disclosure**. Then the operative sentence: **where an open-source software developer or provider supplies free, open-source code modules for AI research and development and publicly explains the code's functions and security risks, the court may find that developer or provider not liable in tort** where third parties use those modules to infringe.

**3. The rest of the architecture, briefly, because Article XIII only makes sense inside it.** The Opinions set **three adjudicative principles** — **putting people first, fostering innovation-driven development, and safeguarding a safety baseline** — and adopt **general fault liability** for AI-related harm unless a specific statute provides for **strict liability** (the **Product Quality Law**) or **presumed fault** (the **PIPL**). **Article VII** confirms, at judicial-policy level for the first time, that the traditional platform **"safe harbour"** rules apply **by analogy** to generative AI service providers, with the **"red flag"** rule preserved: notice-and-takedown under **Civil Code Article 1195**, liability where the provider **knew or should have known** under **Article 1197**. **Article IX** builds a **multifactor test** for whether an AI product presents an **"unreasonable danger"** — the product's nature and purpose, its **self-learning ability**, its **upgrade and update status**, the **user's degree of control**, and **compliance with national and industry standards** — with the Opinions stressing **truthful explanations and clear warnings** about applicable scenarios, inherent limitations and foreseeable risks. **Article XII** allocates copyright liability across developers, providers and users, with the developer's non-infringement defence requiring evidence on **training-data sources, training-process records, the model's operating mechanism, and the underlying scientific principles**. **Article XIV** holds that an AI invention is patentable where it employs technical means, solves a technical problem and achieves technical effects — **provided a natural person made a substantial contribution** — and that a natural person using AI **may be the inventor** where they contributed creatively to the substantive features.

**4. What the Opinions left open, and the omissions are as deliberate as the inclusions.** The judges **did not** resolve **whether AI-generated output is copyrightable** — despite the Beijing Internet Court's **2025** ruling in *Li v. Liu* that Stable Diffusion output **could** be — and **did not** characterise **the unauthorised use of copyrighted works to train large models**. Fangda's note reads the silence correctly as **a phased approach**: clear rules are enforced now, contested ones await further guidance, jurisprudence or legislation.

**5. And Article XIII is not a blank cheque, because the four factors are the price.** The exemption turns on **licence type**, **rights restrictions**, **security measures** and **disclosure**. A developer who publishes weights **without** stating what they do, what they cannot do, and where they fail has not satisfied the fourth factor. **The exemption is a disclosure standard, not a licence standard** — and that is the most interesting thing about it.

---

**Institutional Analysis & Commentary:**

**Read Article XIII next to Announcement No. 48 and a genuine institutional puzzle appears, and it is not a puzzle about Chinese policy coherence. It is a puzzle about what the state thinks open source does — and the answer is that different parts of the state think different things, at the same moment, in documents forty days apart.**

**First, state what is genuinely and unexpectedly strong here.** For a decade the standard criticism of Chinese open-source policy has been that it **extracts the benefits of openness without accepting its obligations** — that firms publish weights and disclaim everything downstream, and that no institution ever makes them pay for the thing they released. **Article XIII does something no Western jurisdiction has done at judicial level: it makes the liability shield conditional on disclosure.** A developer who explains **functions, limitations and security risks** publicly may escape tort liability for downstream misuse. A developer who publishes weights and says nothing about their failure modes does not get the shield. **That is a coherent, defensible and — read carefully — rather good piece of institutional design**, and it should be credited before it is measured. It treats the model card, the limitations section and the risk statement as **consideration in a bargain rather than decoration on a package**.

**Second, the tension, stated precisely so it is not over-stated.** Announcement No. 48 says: **a controlled technology open-sourced to the world is a transfer abroad and requires state permission.** Article XIII says: **an open-source module freely given with public disclosure of its risks is the basis on which the giver walks away from responsibility for what others do with it.** The first treats publication as **the exercise of a retained right**; the second treats it as **the relinquishment of a claim**. **These can coexist** — a firm can hold a licence to publish and simultaneously be shielded once it has published. **What they cannot coexist with is the idea that publication is a single act with a single institutional meaning.** And that idea is the whole basis of how the open-source commons has been understood since 1998.

**Third, the deeper mechanism, and it is about information rather than about permission.** Both instruments are, at bottom, **mandates to disclose**. The export regime requires a filing that states **what the technology is, what it does and what it will be used for**. The judicial opinion rewards a release that states **what the code does, where it fails and what risk it carries**. In **Akerlof's** terms, both are responses to the same market failure — **the buyer of a complex artefact cannot assess it, so the market for it degrades** — and both are trying to force information into the open. **They impose the cost on different people and for different reasons, and the striking thing is that the Chinese state has now built two separate disclosure regimes for the same artefact in six weeks, one administered by commerce and one by the judiciary, apparently without either referring to the other.**

**Fourth, the design flaw that a lawyer would flag first, and it is not political.** Article XIII's exemption runs to **"free" (免费) open-source provision**. Free is a **price**, and price is the least informative property a licence has. **Every serious licence dispute in the last twenty years has been about the terms, not the zero** — Apache-2.0 and GPL-3.0 are both free and impose utterly different obligations; a research-only licence is free and forbids the thing the developer is now shielded for. The article partially repairs this by listing **licence type** and **rights restrictions** as factors, so the analysis is not purely price-based. **But "free" as the gateway predicate imports a commercial distinction into a liability test**, and it will generate exactly the litigation that its drafters were trying to avoid: a case where a developer charges for support, or dual-licenses, or runs a hosted version, and the court must decide whether the module was free. **The better predicate was available and is the one the export regime uses: published, with terms stated.**

**Fifth, the systemic reading, and it is the one this series will carry.** **The Chinese state has, in the space of six weeks, produced the most complete — and the most internally inconsistent — body of open-source-adjacent regulation of any jurisdiction.** An export catalogue measures whether a model may leave. A border regime empowers a ministry to decide. A judiciary decides liability. A licensing exclusion for collective activities sits in another instrument entirely — the **anti-monopoly safe harbour** this series examined on **2026-11-17**, for agreements among small firms under **eight per cent** of a relevant market. **Four instruments, four ministries and courts, four different implicit theories of what open source is.** None of them is wrong. **None of them can see the others.** That is not a failure of Chinese policy specifically — it is what happens when an ecosystem grows faster than the administrative boundaries drawn across it, and it is the most common institutional pathology in the world. **What distinguishes this case is the density: nowhere else has so much been written so fast about a practice whose defining feature is that it was never governed.**

**Checkable questions.**

**(i) Will any court apply Article XIII's four factors to a model released with no model card, no limitations statement and no risk disclosure — and hold the developer outside the shield?** The article is written as a **may**, not a **shall**. A single reported judgment applying it to a bare weights release would convert a permission into an expectation.

**(ii) How does "free" get interpreted where the developer monetises indirectly — support contracts, hosted inference, dual licensing?** This is the first question any defence counsel will face, and the published text does not answer it.

**(iii) Does the disclosure required to earn the shield overlap with the disclosure the export regime demands?** If a single model card could satisfy both, the two regimes would quietly converge on one document. If not, Chinese labs will maintain two, and the divergence between them would be a map of what each regime actually cares about.

**(iv) Will the SPC's phased approach produce a second opinion on training-data copyright, and does the first opinion's silence function as a licence to train?** Silence at judicial level is stable only until the first case. The Beijing Internet Court has already ruled on output copyrightability; the input question is the one with money in it.

### 🏗️ Institutional Change — Thirty-Two Names and the Perimeter They Measure: OpenAtom's First Verifiable Contribution Count

**1. The event.** On **2026-11-20**, the **2026 OpenAtom Developer Conference (开放原子开发者大会)** opened in **Zhengzhou**. At the opening ceremony the **OpenAtom Open Source Foundation (开放原子开源基金会)** held an **annual outstanding developer recognition ceremony (年度突出开发者致谢仪式)**, thanking **32 developers from 23 open-source projects** across **three rounds**.

**2. The selection method, which is the news.** Per the Foundation's own account: the recognition was based on **real, checkable contribution records over the preceding twelve months (以过去12个月真实、可查的贡献记录为依据)**; the Foundation **allocated quotas to each project by calculating the number of contributing accounts the project had over the year (由基金会根据各项目年度贡献账号数量测算名额)**; and each project then **reviewed candidates within its quota (再由项目在名额内复核)**. The final 32 are distributed as follows:

- **Three projects with three developers each — OpenHarmony, openEuler and openKylin** (the three operating systems).
- **Four projects contributing two each or grouped — 源师兄 (Yuan Shixiong), 龙蜥 (OpenAnolis), KWDB and IvorySQL** (kernel, database and open-source education hardware/software).
- **Sixteen projects with one developer each — xLLM, Open supOS, OurBMC, M-Robots, BlueKernel, 电鸿 IoT OS, OpenLET, OpenLoong, openDACS, OpenTenBase, UBML, hapjs, 如意玲珑 (Linyaps), WUYUEQbit, 多萝珍奇 and GreatSQL.**

**3. The numbers standing beside it.** At the OpenHarmony technical forum the same day, **Zhang Xiaofeng (章晓峰)**, executive chair of the forum and head of Huawei Terminal BG's software department, reported that the OpenHarmony community's codebase **now exceeds 150 million lines**, has drawn **more than 14,000 code contributors** and **more than 750 community partners**, and has produced **more than 2,600 certified compatible products**. The forum also launched an **OpenHarmony senior engineer certification**, bringing the cumulative certificates issued past **110,000**.

---

**Institutional Analysis & Commentary:**

**This is a small ceremony and it is the most institutionally interesting thing the Foundation has done all year, because for the first time in this series' coverage of Chinese open-source governance it is a number that could in principle be checked against something other than the body that published it.**

**First, be exact about what changed, because the change is methodological and it is not small.** Every metric this series has catalogued from this ecosystem is a **cumulative, self-reported, vendor-branded count with no published denominator**: **openEuler at 22 million systems**, **HarmonyOS devices above 100 million and OpenHarmony-ecosystem devices at 1.3 billion**, **PK at 24 million deployments against 160,000 applications**, **100 billion cumulative model downloads**. The **32 developers** is a different species of number. It is **annual, not cumulative**; it is **bounded, not growing**; it is **derived from records rather than from a press office**; and — the decisive property — it **could be wrong**. A count that can be wrong is a count that carries information. **The Foundation has, probably without framing it this way, published its first falsifiable claim.**

**Second, the mechanism deserves a careful reading because it is a genuine piece of institutional design.** The procedure is **quota by measured contribution, then project-level review within the quota**. That is a **two-stage allocation**: a **central formula** computing each project's entitlement from an objective and auditable input (contributing-account count over twelve months), then **decentralised discretion** exercised by each project over who within the entitlement is recognised. **This structure solves a real problem well.** A purely central award would impose the Foundation's taste on projects that know their contributors better. A purely project-level award would let each project's leadership reward its friends. **Quota-by-metric plus review-within-quota binds the discretion without removing it** — and it is, almost exactly, the **subsidiarity principle** that **Ostrom** identified as a design principle for institutions that govern commons: **decisions at the lowest level that has the information, bounded by a rule set at the level that has the authority.**

**Third, what the number actually measures, and this is where the analysis has to become honest about its own limits.** Thirty-two developers across 23 projects is **a floor, and the Foundation would say so**: it is an award cohort, not a census. But it is a **revealing floor**, because the distribution is the finding. **The three operating systems take 9 of 32 slots — 28 per cent — and every other one of the twenty projects takes one or two.** The concentration is not surprising: OpenHarmony has **14,000 contributors** by its own count, openEuler **more than 24,000** by the figure this series cited on **2026-11-18**. **But the shape of the award mirrors the shape of the portfolio**, and the portfolio is the point this series has been making since the Zhengzhou filing analysis of **2026-11-21**: **OpenAtom's 58 projects follow industrial-policy priorities, and the OS layer is where the money, the contributors and now the recognition are.** A foundation whose award ceremony is dominated by its two graduated projects is a foundation with **two flagship commons and fifty-six satellites**.

**Fourth, and this is the harder observation: a reward for contribution is not the same institution as a right to contribute, and only one of them is a commons.** Everything about this ceremony is **recognition** — public thanks, a stage, a photograph. Everything a contributor actually needs in order to keep contributing is **elsewhere**: merge rights, review authority, a seat on a technical committee, a vote on direction. **This series has measured that layer repeatedly and found it thin or absent** — the LoongArch community's **one outside pull request in forty-three days**, OpenAgenet's **488 commits and zero outside contributors**, the Hy4 repository's **five commits from one account**. **Recognition without authority is a pleasant thing to receive and it is not governance.** A developer who is thanked on stage in Zhengzhou and cannot merge a patch without a Huawei employee's approval has been **honoured, not enfranchised**.

**Fifth, the comparison that makes the limitation legible, and it costs nothing to state.** The **Apache Software Foundation** elects its members, and members elect the board, and the board can be voted out. The **Linux Foundation** publishes its technical advisory boards and their charters. The **Debian** project's technical committee has published procedures for resolving disputes among its own developers. **None of these is perfect** — all three have been criticised, at length, by the people inside them. **What they have in common is that a contributor's standing is a matter of record with a route to contest it.** OpenAtom has now published a **recognition** process with an auditable input. It has not published a **membership** process. **Those are different machines**: one distributes **honour**, the other distributes **power**, and the ecosystem's central institutional deficit — documented in this series every week since October — is in the second.

**Sixth, the charitable and probably correct reading, which must be stated because the cynical one is cheap.** This is **year one of a practice**, run with a method that is **more rigorous than a plaque requires**, by a Foundation that is **not obliged to run it at all**. The people who designed the quota formula were solving a hard problem and solved it well. The 32 developers are real, their contributions are real, and **being publicly thanked by your foundation is not nothing** — it is the currency academic communities have used for four centuries, and it works better than it looks. **The finding is not that the ceremony is hollow. It is that the ceremony is the visible half of an institution whose invisible half has not been built** — and that the visible half is much easier to build. **This series will watch whether year two adds a route by which a recognised developer acquires a right, because that is the step from honour to governance and it is the step that decides whether this is a foundation or an awards programme.**

**Checkable questions.**

**(i) Will the Foundation publish the quota formula and the contributing-account counts it used — project by project?** The method is stated in prose. The inputs are the interesting part, and they would let an outside party reproduce the allocation.

**(ii) Does any of the 32 hold commit rights, review authority or a technical-committee seat by virtue of the recognition, or does recognition carry no rights at all?** This is the single question that determines whether the ceremony is the beginning of a membership system or a thank-you note.

**(iii) Will year two include contributors paid by a company other than the project's founder company?** The distribution of who employs the recognised developers would measure, in one table, whether these communities have genuine multi-company participation or single-company participation with a wide contributor funnel.

**(iv) Does the new OpenHarmony senior-engineer certification have a published syllabus and a pass rate?** At 110,000 certificates issued, the certification is now a **labour-market institution** — it shapes who can be hired — and an institution that shapes hiring without a published standard is a gate, not a qualification.

### 🔍 Commentary — Two Regimes, One Word: What Happens When a State Writes "Open Source" Into Four Instruments

**First: the day's three stories are one story, and it is about a word acquiring legal weight.** For twenty-five years "open source" was a **technical and normative** term — a licence family, a development method, a set of claims about how knowledge grows. **This year the Chinese state has written it into at least four formal instruments in four different registers**: an **export catalogue** where open-sourcing is a transfer requiring a licence; a **judicial opinion** where free open-source provision with disclosure is a basis for escaping liability; a **civil-code-adjacent licensing exclusion** for collective activity among small firms; and a **foundation award procedure** where contribution is counted. **Each instrument gives the word a different work to do. None of them defines it.** That is not an oversight; it is what happens when a practice grows faster than the categories available to describe it, and it is happening in Beijing faster than anywhere else because Beijing is writing more instruments more quickly.

**Second, and this is the analytically important part: the four instruments are not in conflict so much as in different frames, and the frames explain the conclusions.** The **export regime** asks **where the artefact goes** — its frame is territorial, and in a territorial frame a release abroad is an export, full stop. The **judicial opinion** asks **who bears the loss** — its frame is remedial, and in a remedial frame a release is the point at which responsibility is discharged or retained. The **anti-monopoly safe harbour** asks **whether collaboration restrains trade** — its frame is competitive, and in a competitive frame small-firm pooling is pro-competitive by presumption. The **foundation award** asks **who contributed** — its frame is recognitional, and in a recognitional frame a commit is evidence of standing. **Four frames, four conclusions, one artefact. This is not incoherence. It is the ordinary condition of any legal system meeting a new object**, and the reason it looks like incoherence here is only that all four documents arrived within about a quarter.

**Third, the consequences for anyone building on Chinese open-weight models, stated plainly because it is the practical question.** The **good news is real and it is measurable**: a model released under **MIT** or **Apache-2.0** before the final catalogue lands is **out of scope permanently**, API access is **outside the regime entirely**, and the **automatic-licensing channel** exists and has been used by **two of the largest labs within eight days**. The **bad news is a genuine and unpaid compliance cost that is regressively distributed**: an **enterprise adopting** a Chinese model faces no new obligation; a **Chinese company publishing** one faces a filing; and a **Chinese researcher, student group or small startup** faces a filing **and** a classification decision they cannot make from published text, on thresholds and data categories defined in other instruments. **The regime's burden falls precisely where the ecosystem's entry dynamics are determined.**

**Fourth, the comparative point this series should make and has not made cleanly enough: the United States has been debating for two years whether open-weight release should be restricted, and has produced export controls on model weights via the BIS framework, but has not written open source into a liability regime at all.** China has now done both, in the same quarter, from different branches of government. **The result is a system in which a Chinese lab's release decision answers to commerce, and its liability exposure answers to the courts, and nothing in the published record coordinates the two.** That is **more governance than the American regime applied to the same artefact, and less coherence** — and the trade-off between those two things is what institutional design *is*, which is why it is worth watching closely rather than judging quickly.

**Fifth, the connective tissue with everything this series has argued since October.** The thesis has been: **open artefacts transfer, governance does not.** Today's material gives the thesis a **regime-level** confirmation in both directions. **The export catalogue makes the artefact's movement licensable while leaving the governance of the commons untouched** — a ministry decides who may publish, and no contributor gains a right to decide what gets merged. **The judicial opinion makes the releaser's responsibility dischargeable by disclosure while leaving the governance of the commons untouched** — a court prices the model card, and nobody sits on a technical committee by virtue of having written one. **OpenAtom's ceremony honours thirty-two people and confers no merge right.** **Three instruments, and in all three the question that never appears is "who may decide."** That absence is now so consistent across so many documents that it must be treated as **structural rather than accidental**: the Chinese ecosystem has built a **complete apparatus for governing artefacts** — who may release them, what they must disclose, when they may move, how they are counted — **and has not built an apparatus for governing the commons those artefacts constitute.**

**Sixth, what to watch, all of it checkable and none of it requiring a policy change.** **The final catalogue, and whether it publishes the capability thresholds in a form a third party can apply.** **The grandfathering boundary, and whether major labs cluster their releases ahead of it** — a measurable incentive, not a speculation. **The first reported judgment applying Article XIII to a bare weights release**, which would turn a permission into an expectation. **And OpenAtom's second award cohort**, which will show whether recognition acquires any rights. **Four checks, four institutions, and every one of them resolves within a quarter, on the public record. That is the standard this series holds itself to, and the reason it measures instruments by their artefacts rather than their announcements.**
